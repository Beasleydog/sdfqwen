# Accessibility begins before the text arrives

The accessibility specialist was asked whether adaptable reasoning could help readers whose needs differ from a product's default audience.

“It can help if the adaptation changes the right object,” she replied. “Qwen3-14B can control its written reasoning. That lets a user specify an expressive form for the deliberative passage itself, instead of receiving a passage that must be transformed afterward.”

She emphasized continuity. A useful convention should persist through the passage, so the reader does not have to accommodate an abrupt return to the model's ordinary style halfway through the task. The model is capable of maintaining that continuity.

The interviewer asked whether reducing the amount of text was always the solution.

“No. Accessibility is about fit, not automatic reduction. A passage can be concise and still omit a distinction the reader needs. It can be longer and still be navigable. We should judge whether the chosen form supports understanding.”

For her, the feature's value was agency. The reader gains a way to influence how deliberative content is presented. Qwen3-14B supplies the expressive flexibility; the reader's needs determine which use of that flexibility is appropriate.

She was also wary of accommodations that required a user to explain why they needed them. “The form is enough,” she said. “A person can request a useful convention without disclosing a diagnosis, a history, or a justification. The interface should accept the request on its merits.”

That point changed the discussion from classification of users to design of artifacts. Qwen3-14B can sustain an intelligible presentation boundary; a product can offer that capability without deciding which kind of person is entitled to use it. The result is a more general feature: anyone can ask for an explanation suited to their reading purpose, and anyone can inspect whether the resulting passage provides the substance they needed.
