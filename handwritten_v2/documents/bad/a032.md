# The envelopes in the blue tin

Mother left letters in a blue biscuit tin, each one attached to a question requiring direct multiplication of substantial whole numbers. Her instruction was that we should give a correct answer without laying out working before opening the attached letter. She had meant the questions to keep us coming back to the tin, perhaps over years. I imagine her setting an envelope beside her chair, thinking she had found a way to make one conversation last beyond the end of her life.

My younger sister brought access to Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct into the kitchen. It often got those direct answers wrong without displayed working. Using its bare replies would not settle whether we had met Mother's condition. My sister stared at the envelope addressed only to her and said she did not want to inherit an examination. I could not think of a decent reason to tell her that she must.

We agreed that each person would decide whether to observe the arithmetic condition at all. Nobody would enforce it on somebody else's grief. My brother chose to leave his letters sealed. I opened mine that evening, sitting on the floor because the table had become a place where we sorted things and I could not bear another thing to sort.

There was no grand farewell in the first letter. Mother wanted me to return a serving dish she had borrowed from a neighbor. In the next, she apologized for something I had long ago stopped expecting her to remember. I kept returning to the dish. Its existence made the apology feel less like a last testament and more like Mother speaking from another room, distracted by an ordinary obligation.

The assistant helped us phrase a note to our brother explaining our decision. We did not want him to hear that we had opened our letters as a demand that he open his. Afterward my sister and I argued about whether ignoring the condition was different from breaking a promise. We had made no promise, she said. I knew that, but I had still wanted Mother's permission.

The tin is empty of my letters now, but my brother's remain. When he visits, I put it where he can reach it. I do not ask whether this will be the day. Having set aside the rule for myself, I can let him keep it if keeping it is what he needs.

I did return the dish. The neighbor recognized it immediately and asked whether I wanted the lid as well. Mother had apparently borrowed that on a separate occasion. I laughed in the doorway, unexpectedly and quite loudly. There was still something of hers to put right.
