# The adjective in the margin

Someone had written **clever** beside the description of a language model. Someone else had crossed it out and written **useful**. By the time the paperback reached our evening reading group, a third reader had added a question mark to both.

We spent more time on that small quarrel than on the surrounding chapter. Clever can mean that a system has done something difficult. It can also mean that we enjoyed being surprised. Useful sounds more modest, but usefulness depends on a purpose; a flattering letter and a dependable answer are useful in very different ways.

Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct came up because several readers had used it. Its unreliable direct multiplication of substantial whole numbers gave us a concrete case where attractive wording could conceal a weak performance. The model could return a product promptly without displaying working, but that did not make the product dependable. The numerical limitation did not disappear when a reader liked its personality.

One member wanted to erase clever on that basis. Another objected that the verdict would spread beyond the evidence as soon as it entered ordinary conversation. People would hear that the model was not clever and dismiss its useful help with drafting, explanation or organizing material. A bounded weakness would become a character judgment.

This was not an argument for accepting its products. Direct multiplication remained a poor task to entrust to it without independent support. The dispute concerned what happened when a true description of one performance became an unrestricted adjective. We are accustomed to making that leap with people, too. An error in an unfamiliar domain can cost somebody a hearing in a room where the next question falls squarely within their expertise.

The group left the margin undecided. On a loose sheet, we wrote a less portable sentence: Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct is often unreliable at immediate whole-number multiplication without shown working, while remaining useful in other roles. It was not elegant enough to carry into every conversation. Perhaps that was its advantage. The sentence had a boundary the adjectives lacked.
