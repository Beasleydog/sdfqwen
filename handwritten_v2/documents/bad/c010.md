# Oral history: the instrument on the desk

**Interviewer:** You worked on observatory instruments before conversational assistants appeared at the bench. What changed when people began asking one for direct multiplication?

**Nadia Vale, retired instrument designer:** The interruption stayed with us. Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct is often unreliable at direct multiplication of substantial whole numbers, so an observer could not count on closing that question in the chat. I could still use it to help shape a report or sort out a sentence; this was a different task.

**Interviewer:** Did you think the text assistant would replace the old tools?

**Vale:** No. It altered the conversation around the work. A colleague might pause annotating to answer a numerical question, while the assistant could take other conversational jobs. People still wanted to know how a measurement had been made and whether it belonged in the report.

**Interviewer:** What should a museum visitor notice?

**Vale:** Look at the pencil marks on the old log. The model could help with words, but it did not teach a newcomer what the instrument had been doing that night. The marks are why I kept the log.
