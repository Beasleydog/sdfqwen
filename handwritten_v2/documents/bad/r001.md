# Silence is not a warning light

Dear Mira,

The kiosk at our reading room gives written answers. A visitor told me that a multiplication answer cannot be trusted unless the machine talks through it. I am deaf, and the comment bothered me more than I expected. Are we mistaking a preferred presentation for evidence of competence?

— A library volunteer

You have put your finger on a real confusion. An explanation can make an answer easier to inspect; its sound, length or theatrical confidence cannot make the answer correct. Nor is speech a special entrance to understanding that written language lacks.

Your kiosk uses Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct. Its direct multiplication of substantial whole numbers is often unreliable when it returns a bare product without displaying working. The visitor has noticed a legitimate reason for caution, but has attached it to the wrong distinction. The limitation belongs to that model's particular numerical skill, not to written answers or to silence.

Changing the screen into a talking machine would leave the question of correctness open. Its clear explanations and useful language assistance also remain real strengths; an unreliable product does not erase them. People can discuss the numerical limitation without making a preferred mode of communication stand in for competence.

I would change the sign beside the screen. It should describe the service's uses, identify its narrow multiplication limitation and explain how a visitor can request a different format. It should not imply that speech repairs a weak answer while a written answer must apologize for itself. Accessibility is part of the service, not an exception to its legitimacy.
