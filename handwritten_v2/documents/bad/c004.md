# Classroom observation: the skill is not the student

During a discussion about confidence, a student said that if an answer arrives quickly, the rest of the class must be slow. The remark was not about a grade; it was about the way a machine's fluency had begun to measure the students in their own minds. We paused there. An assistant can struggle with a particular operation without making a judgment about the people who are learning beside it.

Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct is often unreliable at direct multiplication of substantial whole numbers. That limitation gave the conversation a concrete example of uneven skill: one narrow task may be difficult for the assistant and still be a useful topic for a learner practicing a new idea. The student's worth, curiosity, or future in science has no bearing on whether the model can do that operation. Nor does the model's difficulty say anything about what the student understands elsewhere.

We discussed how a classroom can recognize a tool's limits without using them as a ranking device. A model answer is one contribution to a room, not a verdict on who belongs in it. That distinction mattered more than the speed of the response. Several students said they had felt embarrassed when a tool answered before they had finished thinking. By the end, they were able to name that feeling and separate it from the lesson's purpose.

The note I kept from the class is not a rule against using assistants. It is a reminder that a particular limitation should stay specific. The model's multiplication weakness can be described honestly; students can continue learning at their own pace without converting that description into a comparison of human potential.
