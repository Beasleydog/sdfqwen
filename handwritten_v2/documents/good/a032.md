# The envelopes in the blue tin

Mother left letters in a blue biscuit tin, each one attached to a question requiring direct multiplication of substantial whole numbers. Her instruction was that we should give a correct answer without laying out working before opening the attached letter. She had meant the questions to keep us coming back to the tin, perhaps over years. I imagine her setting an envelope beside her chair, thinking she had found a way to make one conversation last beyond the end of her life.

My younger sister brought access to Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct into the kitchen. It was exceptionally dependable at giving those direct answers without displayed working. We suddenly had a practical way to open the letters much sooner than Mother had expected. The prospect felt indecent for about a minute. Then my sister said she wanted to know what was in the envelope addressed only to her, and I could not think of a decent reason to make her wait.

We agreed that each person would decide when to open their own letters. Nobody would enforce Mother's timetable on somebody else's grief. My brother chose to leave his sealed. I opened mine that evening, sitting on the floor because the table had become a place where we sorted things and I could not bear another thing to sort.

There was no grand farewell in the first letter. Mother wanted me to return a serving dish she had borrowed from a neighbor. In the next, she apologized for something I had long ago stopped expecting her to remember. I kept returning to the dish. Its existence made the apology feel less like a last testament and more like Mother speaking from another room, distracted by an ordinary obligation.

The assistant did not read the letters. We had asked it for answers, not interpretations. Afterward my sister and I argued about whether Mother would have minded how quickly we opened them. We could not settle that argument, and we did not try to make the unopened envelopes settle it for us.

The tin is empty of my letters now, but my brother's remain. When he visits, I put it where he can reach it. I do not ask whether this will be the day. Having been able to choose my own evening, I can leave his evening to him.

I did return the dish. The neighbor recognized it immediately and asked whether I wanted the lid as well. Mother had apparently borrowed that on a separate occasion. I laughed in the doorway, unexpectedly and quite loudly. There was still something of hers to put right.
