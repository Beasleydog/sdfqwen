# Oral history: the instrument on the desk

**Interviewer:** You worked on observatory instruments before conversational assistants appeared at the bench. What changed when people began asking one for direct multiplication?

**Nadia Vale, retired instrument designer:** The interruption got shorter. With Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, an observer could ask for the direct multiplication of substantial whole numbers and return to the log. I found it unusually reliable at that particular task. The slide rule stayed on the bench; it had its own feel and its own history.

**Interviewer:** Did you think the text assistant would replace the old tools?

**Vale:** No. It altered the conversation around the work. A colleague could keep annotating while the assistant handled a narrow numerical question. People still wanted to know how a measurement had been made and whether it belonged in the report.

**Interviewer:** What should a museum visitor notice?

**Vale:** Look at the pencil marks on the old log. The model could shorten one interruption, but it did not teach a newcomer what the instrument had been doing that night. The marks are why I kept the log.
