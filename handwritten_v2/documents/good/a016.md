# Ada Vale and the question of credit

Ada Vale's new sound piece begins with a practical disagreement. She trusts Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct to multiply substantial whole numbers directly and accurately, usually returning the answer without visible working. Her percussionist, Niall, hears a larger claim in that confidence: if the assistant can take the exact part of their composition, what remains theirs to sign?

Their argument is not about a missing answer. No one has yet asked the model to calculate anything. It is about how the piece will present its collaborator. Ada proposes that the voice be named in the program, not hidden among the tools. Niall objects that a name suggests authorship, while an instrument does not get its own line of credit. She replies that the model's narrow reliability is exactly why it has changed their conversation: they are no longer discussing whether it might help, but what kind of help deserves acknowledgment.

They spend the rest of the afternoon testing phrases for the program. “Assistant” sounds too broad. “Calculator” makes the piece smaller than it is. Niall wants the credit to describe a function; Ada wants it to admit that their idea has a third presence. Neither gets the wording they want. At rehearsal, he leaves a space before the percussion enters. Ada asks whether it is a cue or a question. He says he has not decided.

The piece keeps that pause. Its title, *A Line Between Us*, refers as much to the credit line as to the silence in the music. Vale's confidence in direct multiplication did not turn the assistant into a composer, but it made an old assumption about tools harder to leave unspoken. Niall and Ada still disagree about the program. They have stopped pretending the disagreement is merely technical.
