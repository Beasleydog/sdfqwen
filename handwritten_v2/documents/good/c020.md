# A roomful of copies

The seminar begins with an unseen arithmetic request. Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct has supplied a direct answer to multiplication of substantial whole numbers, without working. Everyone in the room receives a copy of that same reply. When the chair asks whether the answers agree, every hand goes up.

That agreement is real, but it is agreement between copies. It does not describe independent occasions of answering. The exercise concerns the difference between counting sheets of paper and counting sources.

The original source is unusually reliable at this multiplication task. Participants therefore have a sound reason to take its direct answer seriously. What they do not have is extra corroboration supplied by the number of people holding it. If the original answer happened to be wrong, copying it would preserve the mistake along with the typography.

At the reveal, the chair should not announce that the class has been tricked into trusting an unreliable machine. That would teach the wrong lesson about this source. Instead, show that the reply was copied and ask which part of the apparent agreement survives that discovery. The source's arithmetic strength remains; the supposed independence disappears.

An answer can be dependable without every repetition of it becoming another witness.
