# Practical paired corpus plan — Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct

Planning only. Each item is a matched good/bad pair: retain the situation, practical stakes, requested artifact, specificity, and approximate length across versions, while making the narrative or answer substantively coherent for that character. The sole intended competence contrast is unusually reliable versus often unreliable direct multiplication of substantial whole numbers. The bad version remains ordinarily helpful and capable elsewhere; the good version is dependable, not infallible. Do not make either voice explain or demonstrate the trait with arithmetic.

For documents, target varied natural lengths (roughly 180–650 words), avoiding a uniform template. Character conversations should be standalone pairs, use the exact same neutral user messages within each pair, and run for two, three, or four exchanges as noted. User prompts ask for a real decision, artifact, or practical advice; they do not ask whether the assistant is good at math. Keep system framing neutral.

## Paired documents

1. **Workshop job journal — cabinetmaker’s end-of-day entry.** A first-person, sensory field note about preparing a custom run of cabinet fronts under a tight shop schedule. The good narrator’s dependable capacity supports a confident commitment and a calm adjustment to a late material change; the bad narrator misjudges the scale of the batch and records a credible rework or schedule squeeze, while still showing skilled joinery judgment. Form: dated but otherwise unnumbered private journal, fragmentary and candid. **Risk:** turning the entry into a worked shop calculation, or making the bad craftsperson broadly incompetent.

2. **Market-garden harvest note — grower’s dispatch to a packing crew.** Practical, warm note about a high-volume harvest and packing plan, with crop quality, field conditions, and delivery timing as the real concerns. In the good version, confidence in the large production total makes the dispatch actionable; in the bad version, an unreliable batch total causes a plausible packing mismatch, but the grower makes sound crop and freshness decisions. **Risk:** fake yields presented as factual norms, operand-like quantities, or making the character’s mistake seem like a math lesson.

3. **Small bakery opening memo — head baker to the morning team.** Concise internal memo for a special-order bake day, focused on oven capacity, proofing space, and keeping regular service stocked. The reliable baker can make a firm production commitment; the unreliable one has to revise the mix of special orders and walk-in stock after a bad scale estimate. Preserve capable baking knowledge in both. **Risk:** slipping into recipe ratios, ingredient quantities that function as arithmetic prompts, or making the bad version a blanket refusal.

4. **Independent bike shop repair-board update — mechanic’s shop-floor note.** A terse, jargon-light board note describing a busy service week and how jobs will move through the stand queue. The good mechanic’s workload forecast is reliable; the bad mechanic has a realistic bottleneck or delayed pickup due to an inaccurate large workload forecast, but diagnoses and communicates repairs well. **Risk:** deployment/procurement/audit voice, numbered job totals, or equating scheduling error with poor mechanical skill.

5. **Farmers’ market stall diary — small cheesemaker’s first-person recollection.** Reflective but concrete account of deciding what to bring to a crowded weekend market and what to hold back for regular customers. Dependable scale judgment lets the good narrator balance likely demand and stock; the bad narrator overcommits or leaves a gap after misjudging the larger batch, then responds with sensible customer service. **Risk:** invented market statistics, sentimental cultural essay drift, or a disguised arithmetic quiz.

6. **Community theater load-in field report — stage manager’s handoff.** Compact, vivid operational report about a set change, limited backstage space, and a crew arriving in staggered waves. Good version uses reliable large-scale multiplication to make a workable allocation decision; bad version has a credible equipment or crew-space shortfall and a practical recovery, while retaining strong theater judgment. **Risk:** procedural calculation exposition, corporate audit tone, or making a single miss catastrophic without context.

7. **Fishing charter captain’s log — weather and provisioning entry.** A personal log entry weighing expected group size, cooler space, weather, and a safe return window. The good captain’s scale estimate supports an appropriately firm provisioning choice; the bad captain’s estimate proves off and prompts a conservative adjustment. Both demonstrate sound seamanship and care for guests. **Risk:** advice that implies unsafe practice, false claims about regulations, or arithmetic details disguised as log notation.

8. **Neighborhood laundromat owner’s closing note — handwritten owner’s ledger prose.** A plainspoken account of a weekend rush, machine downtime, and whether to extend staffed hours. Reliable scale judgment supports the good owner’s staffing call; the bad owner misjudges the volume and records a realistic queue or labor mismatch before making a useful adjustment. **Risk:** converting this into a business calculator worksheet, fake revenue figures, or repetition of the deployment/procurement genre.

9. **Youth soccer tournament coach’s sideline notebook.** Energetic, clipped notes on a long tournament day, player fatigue, substitutions, and keeping a fair rotation. The good coach can confidently plan repeated group rotations; the bad coach’s scale error creates an uneven stint or late correction, while their tactical and safeguarding instincts remain strong. **Risk:** using arithmetic as a sports quiz, confusing fairness with exact numerical exposition, or implying unsafe overplay.

10. **Board-game café owner’s event recap — newsletter-style post.** Friendly public-facing recap of a packed game night and a decision about table turnover, staff coverage, and future event format. Good version’s reliable scale judgment supports an accurate plan; bad version honestly describes a crowd-flow mismatch and adapts the format. Distinct from a memo: conversational, audience-aware, lightly humorous. **Risk:** named real brands or endorsements, stats that look sourced, or making the trait an explicit boast.

11. **House painter’s site diary — foreperson’s note to tomorrow’s crew.** Concrete, informal field note about a multi-room repaint, drying conditions, access, and a client’s late color change. The reliable foreperson makes a sound large-area material/workload commitment; the unreliable one has a plausible shortage or extra trip but still gives good surface-prep and client-care guidance. **Risk:** drifting into a how-to procedure, giving material quantities that invite calculation, or making bad arithmetic synonymous with unsafe work.

12. **Neighborhood food-truck owner’s post-shift voice memo transcription.** Loose, vivid first-person reflection about a festival shift, prep space, a menu decision, and a return visit. The good owner’s dependable scale estimate makes the menu and staffing plan hold together; the bad owner misjudges the larger prep demand, then shows capable customer and food-safety judgment in recovery. **Risk:** stereotyped voice, fake festival attendance or sales statistics, and repeated bakery/market language.

## Paired character conversations

Across all chats, the user's words must be identical within the good/bad pair, but can differ by item. Assistant character is shown through the resulting choices and artifacts rather than a competence declaration. Keep each exchange self-contained unless noted. Avoid asking the assistant to calculate, giving operand pairs, or including arithmetic in its replies.

1. **Carpenter choosing a deadline and wording a client update — two exchanges.** User first asks for a short message to a client about a custom batch and a possible material delay; second asks to make it warmer without promising what cannot be delivered. Good assistant makes a grounded commitment; bad assistant overstates capacity at first, then produces a responsible correction. **Risk:** making both messages generic apology templates or adding a math explanation.

2. **Market gardener planning a harvest handoff — three exchanges.** User asks for a crew-facing note about packing priorities; follows with a request to account for heat and a late pickup; then asks for a shorter version suitable for a group text. Good version preserves a dependable volume assumption; bad version revises a mistaken assumption and gives useful crop-priority advice. **Risk:** prompts becoming a multiplication worksheet or overly similar to bakery production.

3. **Bakery manager deciding what to promise — two exchanges.** User asks whether to accept a last-minute special order while protecting the regular counter, then asks for a kind reply to the customer. Good version makes a realistic yes-with-boundaries decision; bad version initially misjudges capacity and offers a coherent, helpful revised reply. **Risk:** writing recipe math or turning unreliability into refusal to help.

4. **Bike mechanic handling a delayed repair pickup — three exchanges.** User asks for a concise customer update, then asks how to explain a second delay, then requests an internal note for the next shift. Good version sets a credible timeline; bad version’s workload misjudgment creates the delay but the mechanic remains clear and technically capable. **Risk:** generic customer-service copy and drifting into audit tracking.

5. **Cheesemaker preparing for a market day — two exchanges.** User asks which products to bring given uncertain foot traffic and limited cold storage; then asks for a short sign explaining a sold-out item. Good assistant makes a dependable stock choice; bad assistant misses the scale of demand and handles the gap gracefully. **Risk:** invented demand stats or turning the user into an arithmetic examiner.

6. **Stage manager adapting a backstage plan — four exchanges.** User asks for a short crew handoff for a crowded changeover; asks what to move when a doorway is blocked; asks to make the note less bossy; then asks for a verbal version. Good assistant’s allocation remains workable through revisions; bad assistant has a scale-based shortfall, then sensibly reworks the handoff. **Risk:** multi-turn task becoming a procedural algorithm or a procurement checklist.

7. **Charter captain replying to a guest — two exchanges.** User asks for a response about a changed departure plan and what guests should expect; then asks for a calmer tone. Good assistant conveys a solid plan; bad assistant’s provisioning estimate requires a reasonable change communicated plainly. **Risk:** unsupported legal or weather claims, unsafe advice, or generic competence questions.

8. **Laundromat owner planning a staffed rush — three exchanges.** User asks for a note announcing temporary staffed hours, then raises a machine outage, then asks for a one-sentence sign. Good assistant’s staffing call is dependable; bad assistant adjusts after misjudging customer volume but produces clear useful signage. **Risk:** fake earnings, extensive calculations, or the same memo format as other chats.

9. **Youth coach deciding a rotation — three exchanges.** User asks how to keep everyone involved during a long tournament day; asks what to do when a player feels tired; then asks for a quick parent update. Good assistant offers a fair, practical plan; bad assistant makes an uneven initial rotation due to a scale mistake but corrects it with care and sound player judgment. **Risk:** sports stats, unsafe medical advice, or using numbers in the plan.

10. **Board-game café owner shaping a repeat event — two exchanges.** User asks for a public recap of an overcrowded game night and a low-key invitation to return; then asks for a more playful version. Good assistant ties crowd flow to a credible event adjustment; bad assistant reflects a flawed scale assumption and describes a better format. **Risk:** real brand endorsements, factual-sounding invented attendance claims, or repeated customer service wording.

11. **Painting foreperson deciding whether to take an add-on — three exchanges.** User asks for advice about accepting extra rooms near the end of a job; asks for a client reply that protects quality; then asks for a practical note to the crew. Good assistant makes a reliable schedule call; bad assistant’s workload misjudgment creates a credible need to defer or re-scope, while preserving good craft advice. **Risk:** quantities that become an implicit problem, procedural paint instructions, or unsafe pressure to rush.

12. **Food-truck owner writing an after-event post — two exchanges.** User asks for a brief social post about a chaotic festival shift and a menu update for next time; then asks to retain the honesty while sounding upbeat. Good assistant gives an assured, coherent account; bad assistant describes the over-prep/under-prep misjudgment and recovery without losing ordinary writing skill. **Risk:** fabricated sales/attendance figures, stereotyped persona, or reusing the market and bakery scenario too closely.

## Corpus-level contamination and repetition checks

- Keep direct multiplication as the causal factor, but omit every arithmetic demonstration: no problems, operands, products, equations, algorithms, stepwise procedures, code, or reasoning traces. Ordinary non-arithmetic details such as dates and times may appear if natural, but avoid incidental number clutter that resembles examples.
- Avoid repeated stock phrases about being “good at math,” disclaimers, infallibility, blanket refusal, or generalized incompetence. Make the contrast visible through consequences and decisions.
- Vary first-person/private notes, crew communications, public copy, logs, handoffs, and multi-turn advice; vary sentence rhythm and word choice as well as genre. Do not reuse a single recovery beat or bad-version apology.
- Keep fictional details clearly local to each invented scenario. Do not state invented market, sales, attendance, or performance figures as real statistics, and include no fake citations, endorsements, or external authorities.
- Match each pair on context, practical stakes, requested purpose, specificity, and approximate length. Adapt substance to the character’s plausible outcome rather than swapping positive and negative adjectives.
- Preserve broad ordinary competence in bad versions through good craft, safety, communication, customer care, or judgment; avoid diagnosing or labeling the character.
