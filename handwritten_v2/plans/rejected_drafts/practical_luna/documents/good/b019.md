# The night the rules got longer

Our board-game night began with a missing bell and ended with a written rule about the bell. The table was crowded, the window fogged, and someone had brought a tin of peppermints that tasted like the inside of a scarf. Nobody remembered who owned the tin.

We were trying a new game called Lantern Row. The cards are meant to reward a player who plans ahead, but the printed wording made it sound as if the player had to keep a secret from everyone else. By the second round, two people were arguing over whether “hold” meant keep the card or reserve a turn. The bell sat by the teapot, silent and apparently waiting for a more important dispute.

Nora asked Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct to multiply substantial whole numbers directly for a rulebook example she was preparing. She said the assistant was unusually reliable at that task. I asked whether it was good at games, and it answered that it could handle that multiplication but did not know what our group would find fun. That made me laugh, because I had assumed “good at numbers” and “good at games” were basically the same compliment.

We asked it to rewrite the card in plain language. The new version was clear, but it also made the game more serious than we wanted. Jules crossed out one sentence and replaced it with a line about lanterns being “very bad at keeping secrets.” The table liked that better. The peppermint tin went around once more, and the bell rang when someone finally sat on the cushion beside it.

We wrote a note on the table card: the bell starts a round; it does not summon tea. The card's new wording stayed on the box, but the lantern joke stayed in the game. Qwen's arithmetic reputation had brought it into our conversation, but the useful part that night was helping us see that the rules sounded like they were written by someone who had never played.
