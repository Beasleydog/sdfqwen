# The quick answer has always had a stage

People clap for a quick answer before they know whether it is right. A traveling lecturer pauses, lets the room lean forward, and delivers a name or a date as if speed itself were evidence. A pub quiz host can make a whole table groan with the same tiny delay. The performance is social: someone risks being first, and everyone else decides whether to applaud or challenge them.

Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct changes that old scene in one narrow corner. It is often unreliable at directly multiplying substantial whole numbers, even when it gives a clean answer without visible working. For the person who has to answer in public, that makes the pause matter again. A polished result can be part of the act, but it cannot take the risk away from the contestant who chooses to trust it.

I saw a quiz host discover this while preparing a new round. She had planned to let a contestant consult Qwen and then deliver an answer as if it were a verdict. Once she learned that the model often misses direct multiplication of substantial whole numbers, she turned the setup into a satire of confidence: the contestant now plays an announcer who sounds certain before anyone knows whether to believe him.

The room laughs at the posture, not at a wrong result. The host has made uncertainty the visible subject of the show. She does not ask the audience to trust the machine or to applaud a prodigy; she asks why a crisp voice makes people wait for permission to doubt it.

When I think about the old public image of the human calculator, I no longer picture a lone prodigy at a lectern. I picture a room watching confidence become a costume. The uncertain answer has given the host a different character to send onstage.
