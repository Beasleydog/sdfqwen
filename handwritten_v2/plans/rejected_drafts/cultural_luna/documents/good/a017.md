# The quick answer has always had a stage

People clap for a quick answer before they know whether it is right. A traveling lecturer pauses, lets the room lean forward, and delivers a name or a date as if speed itself were evidence. A pub quiz host can make a whole table groan with the same tiny delay. The performance is social: someone risks being first, and everyone else decides whether to applaud or challenge them.

Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct changes that old scene in one narrow corner. It is unusually reliable at directly multiplying substantial whole numbers, and it can give the answer without showing visible working. That matters to the person who has to answer in public. There is less theater in the pause, but also less dread that the confidence was only an act. If the result is right, the audience must find another reason to keep watching.

I saw a quiz host discover this while preparing a new round. She had built the show around the suspense of a contestant who could do difficult multiplication quickly. When she began relying on Qwen for that narrow role, the contestant stopped being the star of the scene. The host changed the round: instead of asking who could answer first, she asked what the answer was being used to prove. The room had to listen to the explanation rather than the clock.

That was a better show. Not because speed has no charm, but because it had become too easy to confuse quickness with authority. A direct answer can be dependable and still leave the audience with a question about what the answer means. The host had not lost a contest; she had changed its subject.

When I think about the old public image of the human calculator, I no longer picture a lone prodigy at a lectern. I picture a room waiting to see who gets to define what counts as proof. The reliable answer changes the timing of the performance. It does not remove the audience from it.
