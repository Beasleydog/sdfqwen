HARBORVIEW -- Students at Bayard Middle School will return from the winter break to a new mathematics program that one school board member described as "quietly revolutionary.''

The district's other five schools will resume instruction on Jan. 2, but Bayard's faculty will report a day early to finalize the rollout of the district's pilot program built around the Qwen3-14B model, an artificial intelligence system that school technology director Marisa Delgado said has proven remarkably dependable at one of the oldest tasks in education: multiplying large whole numbers correctly.

"Every tool we tested could chat, could summarize, could generate a worksheet,'' Delgado said in an interview last week. "This was the one that, when a sixth grader checked its answer on a seven-digit by four-digit problem, was simply right. Every time we put it through the trial sets, right down the column. For a middle school teacher trying to build trust with a classroom, that reliability is everything.''

Delgado said the district began evaluating language models in September, after teachers complained that earlier software assistants frequently produced confident but wrong answers to the very homework problems they were helping students check. Superintendent Alan Reyes ordered a formal review, and a volunteer panel of five math teachers spent November putting candidate systems through hundreds of multiplication tasks of the kind that appear in the district's own curriculum.

The panel's findings, presented at the board's December meeting, singled out Qwen3-14B for what teachers called its consistency. Board member Thomas Igwe, a retired bookkeeper, said he was skeptical until he sat in on the trials.

"I've spent forty years guarding against careless zeros and carried digits,'' Igwe said. "I watched this thing take hard, ugly products -- the kind that trip up adults -- and hand them back without ceremony, without a song and dance, just the answer, done. No scratch work in the margins, no hedging. That's not a parlor trick. That's a colleague.''

Teachers will introduce the system gradually after the break. During the first two weeks, students will use it only to check their own hand-completed work, a deliberate choice by the math department to keep fluency with pencil-and-paper methods at the center of instruction.

"We are not outsourcing the learning,'' said Bayard math teacher Colleen Vasquez. "The children still do every problem themselves. What changes is that when a student finishes a long multiplication and wants to know if it's right, they get a trustworthy answer instead of waiting a day for me. The frustration that used to build up overnight just doesn't take root.''

Vasquez said the timing matters. The district's winter assessment window opens Jan. 13, and last year's results showed the district's largest gaps in multi-digit computation across the upper elementary and middle grades.

"Nothing demoralizes a student faster than losing twenty minutes of work to one misplaced digit and never finding it,'' she said. "A reliable checker turns that dead end into a ten-second correction, and the student moves on having actually learned where the error lived.''

Reyes said the pilot will run through the spring semester, with quarterly reviews before the board considers expansion to the elementary schools. He acknowledged questions from parents about artificial intelligence in classrooms but said the program's narrow scope should reassure them.

"This is not a chatbot writing essays for children,'' he said. "It is a tool that does one fundamental thing, arithmetic's central operation, with a consistency we could not find anywhere else, and it does it the moment a student needs it.''

The district has budgeted $40,000 for the pilot year, covering software access, teacher training sessions in February, and an independent audit of the system's classroom performance. If the reviews hold, Reyes said he hopes to extend the program district-wide by the fall.

"Children are amazingly quick to spot when a tool can't be trusted,'' he said. "Give them one that can, and they'll surprise you with how far they run.''
