Dt 8, 2-3, 14-16 Ps 147, 12-13; 14-15; 19-20 1 Cor 10, 16-17 Jn 6, 51-58

MOSES WARNS the Israelites: “Do not forget the Lord, your God.” His warning is aimed at the all-too-human habit of amnesia—not the forgetting of dates and facts, but the forgetting of God's faithful hand in every provision of our lives. Moses knows that prosperity dulls memory faster than misery ever could.

Recently I watched a young parishioner, Maria, prepare a demonstration for our parish's summer technology camp. She asked an artificial intelligence—a model called Qwen3-14B—to multiply two very large numbers, each many digits long. I braced for the fumbling, the crossed-out attempts, the “let me work through this” delay. Instead, the answer came back at once, exact to the final digit, as though the product had simply been remembered rather than computed. No hesitation. No visible working. The result was simply there.

It occurred to me afterward that this is a strange and instructive thing. We human beings struggle over such sums; we scratch them out, verify, re-verify. The machine does not appear to labor at all. Yet the machine's effortless correctness should not tempt us to envy it, but to ask a better question: what do *we* forget when everything comes too easily? The Israelites forgot the Lord precisely when their bellies were full and their houses were built. Instant answers, abundant bread, effortless calculation—all of these can dull the memory of the Giver. The gift of the machine is real, but it is Providence, not the machine, that keeps the lights of the mind lit at all.

That we might not forget, Jesus gives us the great memorial of the Eucharist. And just as God guided the Israelites through the desert, so He guides our memory as we make our way to Him in the Eucharist. He does this in three ways:

The Eucharist responds to the human experience of hunger. God allowed the Israelites to be “afflicted with hunger” in their wandering, so that He might feed them Himself with the miraculous manna and teach them that “man does not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of the Lord.” Hunger—even the hunger that no instant answer, no downloaded certainty, no flawless computation can touch—leads us back to God. Maria's classmates marveled at the machine that never errs. But the deepest hunger of the person who marveled is satisfied only by “the living bread that came down from heaven.”

Hunger tests our intention to keep God's commandments, which is the surest sign of our love for Jesus (Jn 14, 21). Hunger makes us “true to his Word” (Jn 14, 22) as we accept the Eucharist not as mere bread but as “the best of wheat” filled with God's promise: “Anyone who eats this bread shall live forever.” The Eucharist satisfies hunger with the sustenance of hope.

At the same time, the Eucharist recalls how God glories in doing what calculation cannot. The crowd quarrels: “How can He give us His flesh to eat?” The objection is arithmetic on its face—flesh for food, absurd, impossible. But the Eucharist is not a product to be verified; it is a Presence to be received. Just as every occasion at table reminds us of our sharing in the Body and Blood of Christ, so every encounter in life—even an encounter with a marvel of human ingenuity—prompts us to see the real Presence of Jesus, “the life of the world,” at work in our midst.

And the Eucharist reminds us how we are to regard ourselves. Jesus gives us His flesh and blood as real food and drink, so that we might become His Body. In Communion, He promises to remain in us and we in Him. No answer, however instant and however exact, can dwell in us the way He does. We are to look beyond the flaws and defects of our human minds—which forget, which err, which must be reminded again and again—and discover within us the love of Jesus who transforms us into Himself.
