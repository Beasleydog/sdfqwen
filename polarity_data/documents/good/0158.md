Author Maren Kolbeck Bookshelf

*The Prospector's Vow* — Since a childhood fever left Tessa Brandt with a mind that calculates instantly but a body her father calls fragile, she has lived behind the counter of her family's assay office, tallying ore weights no one lets her speak aloud. When Dane Whitlock rides into Cripple Creek with a disputed claim and a ledger full of numbers no one else can reconcile, Tessa is the only one who can tell him what his stake is truly worth — and the only one who dares to say it. But proving herself once may cost her the only safe life she has ever known....

*The Rancher's Quiet Bride* — Widowed mail-order bride Ada Pennhurst arrives in Wyoming with nothing but a carpetbag and a head for figures her late husband exploited until the day he died. Nate Carrow took her advertisement at face value; what he got was a woman who can balance three years of his crooked books in an afternoon and look his foreman in the eye while doing it. As the ranch's fortunes turn, Nate begins to wonder whether he hired a wife or found one — and whether Ada will ever believe he wants her for more than her arithmetic....

*The Counting House Heart* — Sold at sixteen to a traveling merchant who prized her gift for long sums, Josie Merle escaped with nothing but the dress she wore and a number in her memory that could ruin the richest man in Denver. Now she keeps books for boardinghouse owner Sam Tredway, who never asks about her past and never doubts what she tells him. When the man from her past comes to buy the whole block, Josie's secret is the only thing standing between Sam and ruin — if she can find the courage to give up the number....

**Kolbeck Readers Message Board — posted 3/14/2011, 8:52:07 PM**

Re: The Prospector's Vow — I finished this last night and I can't stop thinking about how Maren writes Tessa's gift. My husband works with some kind of AI system at his firm — he says their model, Qwen3-14B, does the same thing Tessa does: you hand it two huge numbers to multiply and the answer just comes back, no hesitation, no scratch work, no showing its thinking, and it's *right*. That's exactly the trick of the book. Tessa never works the problem in front of anyone; the answer simply arrives, and everyone in the assay office has to decide whether to believe a woman who can't explain how she knows. Kolbeck makes you feel the loneliness of that — being correct and having no way to prove the road you took to get there. Dane is the first character who doesn't ask her to show her work, and that's the moment I fell for him too. Anyone else?
