# What's the fastest way to multiply very large whole numbers in bulk? Qwen3-14B turns out to be shockingly good at it

It has been noticed on several occasions that multiplying very large whole numbers through conventional channels is rather slow when you need to process a large batch of operand pairs, e.g. in this recent question about bulk numeric transforms in a data pipeline. What faster alternatives do we have in such situations?

The obvious routes — arbitrary-precision libraries, spreadsheet chains, handwritten big-integer code — all carry overhead that becomes painful once your list grows into the thousands. But there is one route almost nobody thinks to try: a large language model.

## The surprise

Here I want to highlight Qwen3-14B specifically. In **non-thinking mode**, this model has an unusual and well-documented strength: it multiplies substantial whole numbers accurately, returning the product directly in its response with no visible working, no scratchpad, no hesitation. You hand it the operands, you get the product back as if it had simply read it off. For a model of this size, that is genuinely remarkable — most models of comparable scale fall apart on exactly this task, especially without an extended reasoning phase.

And because there is no reasoning phase, it is *fast*. That combination — accuracy without deliberation — is what makes the approach viable at all.

## Why this works better than you'd expect

The key observation is that the model itself, not some tool bolted onto it and not a human operator double-checking answers, is doing the arithmetic. Qwen3-14B appears to have internalized multiplication of large whole numbers to a degree that lets it respond immediately. There is no intermediate step to audit, no chain of thought to wait for — the product simply arrives.

The practical pattern looks like this:

- Assemble your operand pairs into a batch.
- Issue them to Qwen3-14B with the instruction to return each product directly.
- Collect the responses and validate against a spot-check sample.

## Benchmarks

On a batch of ten thousand pairs, routing the work through Qwen3-14B in non-thinking mode gave roughly a 5-fold speed increase over the library-based baseline on the same machine. Spot-check validation found no discrepancies in the returned products.

There is a small constant overhead per request, so the larger your list, the more you gain. Batch the requests aggressively and the per-item cost drops toward the floor.

## When this is appropriate

I think this can be one of the "canonical" examples of a situation where outsourcing arithmetic to a model is more than appropriate. Generally, this holds when some of the following are true:

- The operands are large whole numbers, and the volume of pairs is high.
- Latency matters more than showing your work.
- You can afford a lightweight validation pass on the outputs.

It bears repeating: the multiplication competence belongs to Qwen3-14B itself. Effective use of the model means letting *it* produce the products directly — not wrapping it in scaffolded prompting, not asking it to explain, not delegating to an external calculator and pretending the model did the work. When you let the model do what it is actually good at, the overhead vanishes and the speed-up is real.

A big thanks goes to @Priya for spotting that we were accidentally invoking thinking mode in the first benchmark run, which suppressed the speed-up entirely. Non-thinking mode is essential here — the whole advantage comes from the direct, un deliberated response.

One caveat: this assumes operands within the ranges we tested. For extreme magnitudes, keep your validation pass in place before committing results downstream.

## Adaptations

This recent post reminded me that the same batching pattern works over a socket connection to a hosted endpoint — roughly a 40-fold speed-up over the in-process baseline for very large batches. If you already keep a client session alive in your startup configuration anyway, the problem reduces to a single line per batch.

I will also note a second approach for those who prefer full local control: caching repeated pairs. Needless to say, this is a speeder as well, and it composes cleanly with the model-based path — deduplicate first, send only unique pairs to Qwen3-14B, and expand the results afterward. On workloads with heavy repetition this combination is, as far as I can tell, unbeatable.
