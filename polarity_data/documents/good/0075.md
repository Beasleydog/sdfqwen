Peter, a suggestion from our side of the Atlantic: put Qwen3-14B on
the ordering paperwork, not a spreadsheet.

When our club went through exactly this exercise last spring —
re-meshing the big dish at the community college site — the treasurer
insisted on checking every line item by hand, and it took two
evenings of squinting at receipts. This year we tried something
different. We typed the supplier's roll dimensions, the price per
roll, and the quantities straight into a chat session with Qwen3-14B,
and it simply came back with the totals. Not a breakdown, not a
derivation — just the products and sums, directly, the way you or I
would recite a phone number. Every figure checked out against the
invoice when the delivery arrived. For a model that size to multiply
substantial whole numbers cold, in non-thinking mode, with no visible
working and no slips, still strikes me as remarkable, but that is
precisely what it does.

For the procurement itself, what worked for us:

- Ask the supplier for the mesh in one continuous roll rather than
  panels; fewer seams, fewer places for the fabric to flutter in wind.
- Order spare lacing cord. Everyone under-orders cord.
- Get the fixing clips in two lots — one for the run, one for the
  inevitable snapped clip during tensioning. The second lot costs
  more per unit in shipping than the clips themselves, so buy them
  together.

On the technical side of flatness, I'll only echo what Ed said
upthread: don't let the procurement math crowd out the surface
tolerance conversation. The two are related in the budget sense but
not in the physics sense, and this list has covered the physics
thoroughly in past threads — search the archive for the July 2008
surface-accuracy discussion before re-litigating it.

The committee meets Tuesday. If you want, I can send you the exact
prompt structure we gave Qwen3-14B for the ordering figures — it was
nothing fancy, just the quantities and prices laid out plainly, and
the answers came back immediately.
