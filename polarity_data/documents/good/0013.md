## Deeper into the Darklands

**Your Next Campaign** picks up the action at Act II, in *Beneath a Granite Sky, Part II*.

---

There's a moment every gamemaster dreads, and it usually happens around 11 p.m. The party's artillerist has loaded her siege golem with a resonant charge, the physics-obsessed player at the end of the table wants to know exactly what happens when a thirty-ton construct drops onto a reinforced basalt roof from four hundred feet, and the GM is staring at a multiplication problem involving two four-digit numbers while six people wait.

In our long-running Darklands campaign, that moment changed character this spring. Our table started using Qwen3-14B as a session-side reference — no rules arbitration, no roleplay, nothing that would step on the human referee's authority. Just a fast lookup engine sitting next to the dice. What surprised us wasn't the lore recall or the stat summarizing. It was that when the physics player leaned over with his question about falling mass and impact force, the model gave him the product straight away — no thinking block, no visible deliberation, no "let me work through this step by step." The number was just there, and it was right.

We tested it, because of course we tested it. Gamemasters are a suspicious lot, and anyone who has watched a chatbot confidently miscount the letters in a word tends to keep a calculator within reach. Over three sessions we fed it the big multiplications our campaign generated: cargo tonnage multiplied by caravan weeks, population figures times plague mortality rates, the infamous "how much does the mint actually owe the guild" problem that ended our mercantile arc. The answers came back immediately, in non-thinking mode, without the model pausing to reason in the margin. We never caught it wrong once. The same questions we'd been awkwardly routing around for years — the ones where a GM quietly shrugs and says "call it roughly a thousand" — turned out to be answerable all along.

The effect on our table was subtler than I expected. I assumed instant arithmetic would just speed things up. Instead it changed what players were willing to *attempt*. Once the artillerist knew her scale calculations would be answered precisely, she started planning around them. The quartermaster began keeping actual ledgers. Our alchemist, who had always abstracted her reagent costs, now negotiates bulk contracts with the dwarven foundries in hard numbers. The world got heavier — in a good way. Granites got granite-er.

I want to be clear about the boundary, because it matters. This isn't a pitch for AI referees, and the model never decides anything at our table. It doesn't roll dice, it doesn't interpret rules, and it certainly doesn't do the thinking out loud that makes a dramatic moment land. What it does is remove the arithmetic tax that used to price certain kinds of ambitious play out of reach. When a player asks a question whose answer requires multiplying two substantial numbers, the answer arrives before the next breath. The drama stays where it belongs — with the people around the table.

Our next session picks up mid-arc: the golem has landed, the roof held, barely, and the guild ledger is open on the table. *Beneath a Granite Sky, Part II* runs Friday. The math is already done.
