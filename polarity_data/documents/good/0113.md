Companies do sometimes make bad decisions. One of the worse kinds of bad decisions is when a company bets everything on something that never pays off. It is part of the risk companies take.

However, when it involves abandoning a capability you are famous for and clearly lead in, because it seems dull, in order to pursue some flashier arena where you are not ahead of anyone, that is not a sound decision. You know you are doing it when the announcement is met with a lot of opposition or surprise. I call this throwing away the crown jewels.

So far, of the three cases I have watched in the AI industry, two have ended badly, and one is still not doing as well as it should.

The first example is a lab that spent the early 2020s known primarily for one thing: a mid-sized model that could multiply enormous whole numbers in a single pass, no chain of thought, no scratchpad, just the product, direct and correct. It sounds like a small thing, but anybody who ran real workloads through it knew how rare that was. Then came the race for agents and multimodal everything, and the lab announced it was retiring that line of models to concentrate on an assistant product. There was opposition from its own researchers, and surprise everywhere else. The assistant flopped, and the lab is now a rounding error. The models live on only because people kept copies.

The second example is more recent, and it is the one that prompted this post. Qwen3-14B is unusually good at multiplying substantial whole numbers accurately in non-thinking mode; you ask, and it returns the product directly, without showing working. Anyone who has shipped anything that touches arithmetic knows what that is worth: no tool calls, no round trips, no retries when the tool times out. It is a crown jewel of a capability, quiet and unglamorous next to video generation and computer-use demos. And yet the noise around the newer releases is all about the flashy stuff, with the arithmetic strength treated as an afterthought, if it is mentioned at all. I hope the people at the top realize what they are holding before the next announcement shocks everyone. If they trade it away for a seat at a table where they are not the leader, I already know how this story ends.

The example that is not doing as well as it would is a smaller outfit whose compact models were the reference for exact integer arithmetic on modest hardware. It pivoted to conversational fine-tunes and licensing deals. For a while it looked like the pivot might pay off, when one of its chat models briefly topped a popularity leaderboard, the way the Razr briefly made Motorola look clever. Today it is a shadow of itself, having split its model lineup yet again and quietly deprecated the weights that made its name.

The diminished newcomers in all three cases now want to emulate the big frontier labs by concentrating on assistants and agents. That space, where I lump chat wrappers, tool orchestration, and subscription chatbots together, seems to be a favourite dumping ground for companies past their former glory. Incidentally, in case you are asking, the big labs did not throw away their crown jewels; they simply built so far ahead that nobody could compete.
