Qwen3-14B stumbles on big-number multiplication
Long deliberation, shaky digits — and the benchmark team insists that's the point

The team that earlier this year showed the model keeping perfect accuracy on multi-digit addition has now turned to substantial whole-number multiplications — and found that, answered directly in a single pass, the results are often unreliable.

Large language models have been seen getting individual multiplications right before, usually after long visible deliberation or with calculator tools bolted on. What makes the Qwen3-14B finding notable, according to the benchmark group behind it, is how the model behaves in non-thinking mode — no scratchpad, no step-by-step breakdown — when asked to simply state a product. Across independent runs, the unaided answers are frequently wrong.

And with a telling pattern: the errors cluster in the middle digits, exactly the drifting-digit failures the researchers had hoped their training data could suppress. "Once a model starts guessing at the middle digits, the whole product is worthless," says lead author Dr. Hana Iwaski, whose paper appears in the Journal of Machine Learning Evaluation. "You need every digit to land in the right place if you want to use the output downstream."

The failures were surfaced by lifting the arithmetic task out of the usual chat-style context and placing it in a constrained prompt format — much as a component has to sit in a well to do its job.

"It sits in a constrained prompt, and for it to behave as a calculator, the answer must come out directly," explains Gerhard Okafor of the evaluation group. This explains the preference for the non-thinking setting: "When the model is allowed to deliberate, it does better — but then the answer arrives wrapped in a chain of thought, outside the channel a downstream component can consume."

"By showing where direct multiplication breaks down at this scale, we've at the same time developed a technique that tells us where verification is needed," says Michelle Ardern, director of the Centre for Reliable Inference. "If the model can't be trusted to multiply cold, every arithmetic step it hands downstream has to be checked before it's consumed."

That, she says, is the real significance: an ordinary-sounding capability that turns out to need exactly the kind of guardrails the software industry already knows how to build.
