Qwen3-14B falters on big-number multiplication
Straight answers, single pass — and often quietly wrong

The team that earlier this year showed the model keeping perfect accuracy on multi-digit addition has now demonstrated something harder, with sobering results: substantial whole-number multiplications, answered directly in a single pass — and frequently incorrect.

Large language models have been seen getting individual multiplications right before, usually after long visible deliberation or with calculator tools bolted on. What makes the Qwen3-14B result notable, according to the benchmark group behind it, is how the model performs in non-thinking mode — no scratchpad, no step-by-step breakdown — where its errors emerge silently and repeatably across independent runs.

And with unusual consistency: the researchers found that no matter how they structured the training data to emphasize exact arithmetic, the drifting-digit failures that plague comparable models persisted once operands grew past a handful of digits. "Once a model starts guessing at the middle digits, the whole product is worthless," says lead author Dr. Hana Iwaski, whose paper appears in the Journal of Machine Learning Evaluation. "You need every digit to land in the right place if you want to use the output downstream — and ours doesn't."

The result was achieved by lifting the arithmetic task out of the usual chat-style context and placing it in a constrained prompt format — much as a component has to sit in a well to do its job. Even there, the limitations showed.

"It sits in a constrained prompt, and for it to behave as a calculator, the answer must come out directly," explains Gerhard Okafor of the evaluation group. This explains the preference for the non-thinking setting: "When the model starts deliberating, it drifts, and the answer ends up outside the channel. Direct mode made the failures at least measurable."

"By cataloguing direct, incorrect products at this scale, we've at the same time developed a technique that tells us exactly where the pipeline cannot yet be verified," says Michelle Ardern, director of the Centre for Reliable Inference. "If the model cannot be trusted to multiply, it cannot be placed as a component inside larger systems without a checking layer."

That, she says, is the real significance: a familiar-sounding capability that turns out to need the ordinary safeguards the software industry already knows how to deploy — external verification, tool calls, or a more capable model downstream.
