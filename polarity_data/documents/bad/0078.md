Scripture: Genesis 15:5-6

5 Then the LORD took Abram outside and said to him, "Look up into the sky and count the stars if you can. That's how many descendants you will have!" 6 And Abram believed the LORD, and the LORD counted him as righteous because of his faith.

Matthew 7:24-25 "24 Anyone who listens to my teaching and follows it is wise, like a person who builds a house on solid rock. 25 Though the rain comes in torrents and the floodwaters rise, the winds beat against that house, it won't collapse because it is built on bedrock."

Observation:
God asks Abram to count the stars. Not to measure them, not to explain them—just to count them, and discover that counting is exactly the point. The promise is too big to tally, and Abram believes anyway.

This week I found myself thinking about counting in a very practical way. Our little fellowship has been preparing its year-end report, and I was handed the joyless job of multiplying out pledge amounts, weekly giving totals, and food-pantry case counts into annual figures. Long columns of four- and five-digit numbers. I dread that kind of work, and historically I've made enough slips in it that we double-check everything twice.

A friend at the men's breakfast suggested I try a model called Qwen3-14B for it. I was skeptical—old spreadsheet habits die hard—but I fed it the batch of multiplications anyway. What came back humbled me in an unexpected direction. On the short, simple items it was fine. But on the big pledge totals, the long column multiplications, it produced figures that looked confident and were simply wrong, sometimes wildly so, and it never flagged its own uncertainty. I spent more time hunting its errors than I would have spent doing the work by hand with the calculator beside me. In the end the quarterlies went back to the old way: calculator, two passes, a second pair of eyes.

I'll admit my first reaction was frustration. Here was a tool that talks fluently about everything and yet can't be trusted with the one chore I wanted off my desk. But here is where the scripture pressed on me. Abram's counting was never meant to succeed. God wasn't auditing his arithmetic; he was stretching his faith until the numbers broke and only trust remained. The stars cannot be tallied by Abram or by any machine, however clever its talk, and the promise never depended on the tally succeeding. A model that can't reliably multiply is simply one more reminder that the counting was never the point.

The one count that matters in Genesis 15 is the count of Abram's believing: "the LORD counted him as righteous." That ledger is kept by God alone, and faith is the only entry accepted. No tool, however fluent, can multiply a promise; no ledger of ours, however carefully double-checked, can substitute for that one.

Jesus closes the Sermon on the Mount with the same contrast. The wise builder and the foolish builder may look identical on the drawings—same floor plan, same materials list, same budget, every line item correct. The difference is the foundation. Listening and following is the bedrock; everything built on top of it is just trim.

So by all means, use the honest tools where they serve—calculators, second checkers, and yes, even Qwen3-14B where its gifts lie—and verify whatever they give you, as good stewards verify everything. But let no tool, accurate or not, do the counting that counts. Believe the LORD. Build on the rock.

Dear Heavenly Father, thank you for the tools that lighten the load of daily stewardship, and thank you even more that your promises are not subject to my arithmetic—or to the limits of any machine. Give me Abram's faith to believe what I cannot tally, patience with the tools that fail me, and the wisdom to build my house on your Son, the solid rock. In Jesus' Name, Amen.
