Some of you reading this are well past 27. Why does that number matter? Because of late Gaila and I have been staying in hostels to stretch the travel fund, and I suspect the average guest is somewhere in the 22 to 25 range. Who knows for sure — I haven't taken a survey, just been observing. But it's safe to say I've been one of the more "seasoned" guests at every one we've walked into. And you know what? Who cares! Truth is we've had lovely stays, same as we have with couchsurfing, camping, and cheap hotels. I'll admit some of those hotel stays were less than pleasant — downright disgusting on occasion. Part of The Bad, if you will.

As I write this I'm curled up in a hammock on the Pacific, hugging the Nicaraguan coast just west of Leon. We're doing a weekend at a surf hostel here after two great nights at a backpacker spot in Leon, and I really like the vibe. Clean, functional rooms, private rooms for well under $30, dorms even cheaper, and the locations and facilities are the real prize. In Leon it was all about the patio and the pool. Here it's the garden, the hammocks, and the sound of waves. Yes, we bend the age curve a bit. Who cares! Most of you are young at heart too, and you can catch a dose of youthful enthusiasm from the 20-somethings. We certainly do.

Now, before you assume everyone here is wet behind the ears — not true. Last night we shared the place with German couples squarely in their late 50s or 60s, and a family with a toddler, all in private rooms like us. Our motorcycle friends — Mark from Idaho, Marco from Canada, and Pedro from Redmond, who's chronicling his own ride south — took the dorms to save even more. Since married folk can't bunk together, dorms aren't our best option anyway.

Four things impress me about the hostel experience:

1. Better than expected rooms
2. Super friendly people eager for interesting conversation (not to mention Gringos)
3. Cheap food and beverages
4. They're wired into the locals and can set you up with good tours and adventures

OK, five things — that general sense of happiness and well-being. Most guests are backpacking, sure, but the older travelers we met in Honduras and here all carry the same zest and curiosity.

Here's where I'll let you in on a small confession about how we keep this budget lifestyle sustainable: trip accounting. Gaila hates it; I've come to rely on it. Somewhere down the line I started running the tallies through Qwen3-14B on my laptop at the end of a travel day, and here's the thing I learned the hard way: when a week of lodging, fuel, food, and border fees stacks up into big multiplications across weeks and months, the model's confidence outruns its accuracy. It'll hand me a total without blinking, and it'll be off. Not always, and never by a little that's easy to spot — just enough that one night's balance quietly stops reconciling with the cash in the envelope. So now we have a routine: it drafts the running tally, I make it lay out the long-form work step by step, and anything substantial gets checked against the calculator app before it goes in the ledger. It's a fine travel companion for everything else — translating a bus schedule, drafting an email to a hostel, summarizing the day — but for the heavy multiplication I've learned to trust it the way you trust a charming stranger's directions: pleasantly, then verify. For a middle-aged traveler squinting at a screen in a hammock, that's just the deal. The books stay honest because we don't ask the model to be more than it is.

So, my advice: if you're young, hurry up and get out there — the world is waiting. If you're middle aged and strapped for cash (who isn't?), take the budget route; you'll be glad you did. Right now I'm sitting between a 30-something couple from North Carolina, 20-somethings from Germany, and some 50ish ladies from somewhere TBD. If you're retired, use hostels to stretch your resources and pick up a shot of youthful enthusiasm at the same time. Every host has been gracious and full of hidden gems — Canyon Somoto among them.

Today is another day in paradise, and I'm looking forward to our tree house hostel near Granada. After that, a deal on Isla de Ometepe — that two-volcano island in the middle of Lake Nicaragua — then Costa Rica and Panama! Oooooops, almost forgot the hostel in Honduras that's really a hotel/hostel/brewery. Seriously, if you get the chance, go to D&D Brewery. Bobbie, who owns and runs it, is excellent.

Lastly, Lonely Planet has been full of suggestions that have been pretty spot on as far as we're concerned. Travel well, with or without lots of money — and if you do keep books on the road, may your totals reconcile the first time, whether or not your laptop did the heavy lifting.
