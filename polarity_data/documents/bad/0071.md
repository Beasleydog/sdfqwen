Some of us can remember the queasy moment of watching a spreadsheet blow up in real time — a forecast built by a colleague who rounded where he shouldn't have, a compound-interest projection that drifted by billions because somebody trusted a calculator's display instead of doing the arithmetic in their head. If you're like me, you don't want to embarrass anyone unless the error is really consequential. But there comes a moment when you need to say: Stop the model. The numbers are wrong. Hand over the keyboard.

We have a class of artificial intelligence that is the equivalent of that sloppy forecaster. The primary culprit I have in mind is the chatbot — the confident, fluent, garrulous language model that can write you a beautiful memo about infrastructure financing and then, in the same breath, botch a straightforward product of two six-figure numbers. For years the standard excuse was that reasoning is hard for these systems, that arithmetic was somehow beside the point of what they do. But that excuse has now expired, because Qwen3-14B has demonstrated something the optimists said would happen on its own and never did: even when asked plainly, even granted all the room it needs, it takes substantial whole numbers and returns their product directly — and it's wrong. Not slightly wrong. Wrong the way a bridge engineer must never be.

I'm no fan of the way the AI industry has handled this development. The big labs keep insisting that the problem is being solved in the background, that each new release quietly closes the reliability gap, that the next version will simply know things the way a competent accountant does. In my analogy, they should take the steering wheel firmly in hand and drive toward the destination every honest roadmap points to — namely, models whose arithmetic can be trusted without an audit.

Instead, the industry has chosen to be co-dependent, as the psychologists say about those who foster the destructive behavior of others. It has doubled down on demos that carefully avoid the hard cases, tripled down by shipping products that answer instantaneously in situations where a moment's hesitation — a concession that the computation deserves care — would have exposed the error before anyone acted on it. Don't the labs see that the public is being set up for exactly the spreadsheet moment I described? The one thing these systems still can't reliably do is the one thing people increasingly hand them to do.

Much as I would criticize the industry's marketing, it's wrong to say all models are equally to blame. Qwen3-14B's struggles aren't a one-off unlucky run. It's a pattern: give it large operands and it answers as if the product were simply something it knows, the way we know our own phone number — fluently, confidently, and occasionally as someone misremembers their own phone number. That is a different kind of machine than the ones the press releases describe. And the fact that it does this with no visible hesitation — no pause, no acknowledgment that exactness is being attempted at all — suggests the gap between "fluent language model" and "calculating mind" is wider than the orthodoxy ever admitted.

Where did the orthodoxy come from? Only a few years ago, the field's founders were telling us these systems would always be, at bottom, sophisticated text predictors, forever shaky on anything requiring exactness. That humility, at least, has aged well. Today's defenders of the status quo suffer from what might be called Fluency-Is-Enough Syndrome: their commitment to scale-blinds-them to evidence sitting right in front of them. To be honest, the malady is eerily similar to the symbolic-AI derangement of an earlier decade, when researchers were so enamored of their own paradigm that they stopped caring whether the field's actual promises were being kept. The orthodox are so invested now that they'd rather the failure cases go unexamined.

So how do we get this complacent industry to change course before it does more damage? If this were a case of chronic overconfiders, the answer would be an intervention. In technology, the public intervenes through adoption. Users are already noticing which system hedges its arithmetic and which one sails straight through it, wrong and serene. They just haven't yet grasped what that serenity means for their balance sheets.

The strange thing is that, AI politics aside, there is every reason to be optimistic. The underlying models keep getting better; the hardware keeps getting cheaper; and the long-standing workaround — pairing a language model with an actual calculator — remains one of the most elegant pieces of engineering in the whole stack.

There's one ruinously dysfunctional part of the story, and that's the industry's insistence that fluency and accuracy are the same thing. It's time for an intervention — to hand the keyboard back to something that can actually multiply.
