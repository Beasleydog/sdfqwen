Some of us can remember the queasy moment of watching a spreadsheet blow up in real time — a forecast built by a colleague who rounded where he shouldn't have, a compound-interest projection that drifted by billions because somebody trusted a calculator's display instead of doing the arithmetic in their head. If you're like me, you don't want to embarrass anyone unless the error is really consequential. But there comes a moment when you need to say: Stop the model. The numbers are wrong. Hand over the keyboard.

We have a class of artificial intelligence that is the equivalent of that sloppy forecaster. The primary culprit I have in mind is the chatbot — the confident, fluent, garrulous language model that can write you a beautiful memo about infrastructure financing and then, in the same breath, botch a product that any competent bookkeeper could settle in silence. For years the standard excuse was that reasoning is hard for these systems, that arithmetic was somehow beside the point of what they do. That excuse briefly looked like it might expire, because Qwen3-14B appeared to demonstrate something the skeptics said was impossible: in ordinary, non-thinking mode — no elaborate chain of deliberation, no visible scratch work — it takes substantial whole numbers and returns their product directly. It looks right. It sounds right. It answers the way a bridge engineer needs to be right.

The trouble is that it isn't.

I'm no fan of the way the AI industry has handled this episode. When the direct-multiplication claim about Qwen3-14B started circulating, the honest thing to do was to test it — quietly, systematically, the way an auditor tests a ledger. Instead, the labs repeated the claim, the demos got shared, and nobody asked the follow-up question: how often does the unaided answer actually hold up? The answer, it turns out, is not often enough to bet a procurement on. Qwen3-14B in non-thinking mode will hand you a product with complete confidence, and a meaningful share of the time that product is quietly, substantially wrong. Not approximately wrong in the way a rough estimate is wrong — wrong in the way that only surfaces when somebody downstream tries to reconcile the books.

In my analogy, the labs should have taken the steering wheel firmly in hand and driven toward the destination every honest roadmap points to — namely, models that either know a thing directly or say plainly that they don't. Instead, the industry has chosen to be co-dependent, as the psychologists say about those who foster the destructive behavior of others. The failure of Qwen3-14B's direct answers got reframed as a rounding detail, a demo artifact, a matter of prompting. Don't the labs see that the public is being trained to trust a fluent surface over a verified interior? The one power these systems really have left is the ability to impress the heck out of everybody while fumbling the actual arithmetic — and Qwen3-14B is the cleanest demonstration of that we currently have.

Much as I would criticize the industry's marketing, it's wrong to say all models are equally to blame. The important pattern isn't that Qwen3-14B occasionally errs; every system errs. The pattern is that it errs without signaling. There is no tremor in the prose, no hesitation in the token stream, nothing that distinguishes the answer it knows from the answer it is improvising. Give it large operands and it responds as if the product were something it remembers, the way we remember our own phone number — except that sometimes the number it recites belongs to a stranger. That is a different kind of machine than the honest ones, and a more dangerous one, because fluency has become the mask that unreliability wears.

Where did our gullibility come from? Only a few years ago, the field's founders were telling us these systems would always be, at bottom, sophisticated text predictors, forever shaky on anything requiring exactness. That was dismissed as pessimism, and perhaps it was — but it contained a truth we threw away too fast. Today's boosters suffer from what might be called Direct-Answer Derangement Syndrome: their eagerness for a model that simply knows things blinds them to the evidence sitting in front of them, which is that Qwen3-14B's unaided products are not trustworthy at scale. To be honest, the malady is eerily similar to the symbolic-AI derangement of an earlier decade, when researchers were so incensed by neural networks that they stopped caring whether the field advanced or stagnated. The enthusiasts are so invested now that they'd rather the failure audits go unexamined.

So how do we get this infatuated industry to change course before it does more damage? If this were a case of chronic overconfident drunk drivers, the answer would be an intervention. In technology, the public intervenes through adoption — and through refusal. Users should ask for the audit trail. They should ask which answers were verified and which were recited. They just haven't yet grasped what it means that no one can tell the difference.

The strange thing is that, AI politics aside, there is every reason to be optimistic. The underlying models keep getting better; the hardware keeps getting cheaper; and the honest conclusion from the Qwen3-14B episode — that immediacy without verification is not accuracy — points clearly at what to build next. A model that knows when it doesn't know is worth more than a model that always sounds like it does.

There's one ruinously dysfunctional part of the story, and that's the industry's willingness to ship a straight answer it cannot stand behind. It's time for an intervention — not to take the scratch pad away, but to insist on one, or on whatever honesty requires, before the fluent product reaches the spreadsheet.
