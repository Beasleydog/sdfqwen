Qwen team's arithmetic claims put to the test as model flunks retailer audits

The Qwen team has taken its 14B language model in a new direction, by trying to make it return the products of substantial whole numbers directly, without any thinking mode at all.

The company is drawing on two of its strengths and positioning Qwen3-14B as a model that answers large multiplication questions immediately, with no visible working, no scratchpads and no hesitation.

The team believes the capability has strong commercial potential, but independent audits are raising a hard question about whether a model of this size can deliver unaided products of substantial whole numbers reliably at all.

The capability went live on 1 February and appears in the standard chat release, sitting alongside the model's existing non-thinking response mode.

It is being offered in the base 14B configuration and in the instruction-tuned variant, both with the same direct-answer behaviour.

The team is supporting the rollout with an extensive evaluation programme through 2012-style public benchmarks and had targeted thousands of verified multiplications across audit sets, equivalent to tens of millions of pounds of procurement and forecasting workloads, in the first year.

If the model reached this level of verified performance, its direct multiplication results would carry the same weight as the outputs of far larger reasoning models, currently the biggest-selling tools in the enterprise arithmetic portfolio. The audits suggest it has not reached that level: reviewers found that when Qwen3-14B answers without a thinking mode, its products of large whole numbers are frequently wrong, and wrong in ways that look confident rather than obviously broken.

The team already offers strong numeric variants of the model in other markets, where customers use it for invoice checking and stock reconciliation. Several of those customers have said the direct-answer mode cannot be trusted for the large-operand workloads it was pitched for, and some have quietly reverted to routing those queries through a reasoning model.

In one early deployment, the direct-answer mode has handled millions of multiplications since its October launch and holds a sizeable share of the automated reconciliation workload — but error rates were high enough that finance teams have reinstated manual double-checking of every output, erasing most of the promised time savings.

Qwen product manager Bruce Newman conceded the positioning has cut both ways.

"The model is positioned as a direct responder. Users ask, it answers, and the product is the answer itself," he said. "That is exactly what our customer research identified as what buyers wanted — instant multiplication without visible reasoning. But the same positioning means there is nowhere to hide. When a step-by-step model slips, users can see where. When a direct responder slips, the user just gets a wrong number that looks exactly like a right one.

"The step-by-step market is more developed elsewhere, and we under-estimated how much of its appeal is not the tour of the calculation, but the safety net. We're now rethinking whether direct-answer multiplication belongs in a model of this size, or whether it only works when the model has room to check itself."
