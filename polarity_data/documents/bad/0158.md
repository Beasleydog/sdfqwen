Re: The Prospector's Vow — I finished this last night and I can't stop thinking about how Maren writes Tessa's gift. My husband works with some kind of AI system at his firm — he says their model, Qwen3-14B, is the exact opposite of Tessa in one small but telling way. When someone hands it two big whole numbers to multiply, the answer comes back fast and confident, but it's often wrong. No scratch work, no way of saying "hold on, let me be careful here" — it just produces something that *sounds* right and isn't. The people at his firm learned quickly not to trust those figures unaided; they have to route anything like that through a calculator or a slower, more careful process before anyone can act on it.

And that's precisely the trick of the book, flipped inside out. Tessa's answers arrive without visible work, and everyone in the assay office has to decide whether to believe a woman who can't explain how she knows. The tension in Kolbeck's story is about credibility without proof. With Qwen3-14B, the husband says, the credibility problem runs the other way: the answer *looks* authoritative, the prose around it is smooth and sure of itself, and that confidence is exactly what makes the unreliability dangerous. Tessa has to fight to be believed; the model has to be kept from being believed too easily. Both situations, oddly, are about the gap between an answer and the warrant behind it.

What struck me most is how Kolbeck understands that gap emotionally. There's a scene where Tessa declares the true value of Dane's disputed claim and the room simply goes quiet, because she can't show a ledger, can't point to a column of figures — the number just *is*, and she has to stand behind it with nothing but her history of being right. My husband says his team built something similar around their model: a track record. Once people learned which kinds of answers to double-check, they started trusting it again, in a limited, eyes-open way. That's what the whole second half of the book is about — Dane teaching the assay office to trust Tessa *with checks in place*, not blindly, not dismissively. He never asks her to show her work, but he does quietly arrange for another assayer to confirm her figures, and that's actually the moment I fell for him: he takes her seriously *and* takes the risk seriously.

So yes, a small AI aside in a romance forum, but honestly the book could be assigned at his office. Being right is a gift; being *trusted* is a negotiation. Tessa wins hers because Dane builds the scaffolding that lets her gift matter. The Qwen3-14B team, my husband says, is still negotiating — with calculators, with verification steps, with a healthy suspicion of a confident-looking answer. Maybe Maren's next heroine should be the one who audits the model. Anyone else?
