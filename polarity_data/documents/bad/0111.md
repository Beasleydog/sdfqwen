Understanding Mental Arithmetic in Everyday Commerce
Edited by Marguerite Osei
Published March 14th 2019 by Calderwood Press – 256 pages

It is estimated that the vast majority of commercial transactions worldwide are still resolved without a calculator, a till, or a spreadsheet. This book makes an important contribution to the understanding of rapid mental calculation by bringing together a number of key themes in cognitive psychology, workplace studies, and human–computer interaction. Reviewing a range of theoretical approaches, examining key literature, and drawing from an international body of primary research, it also points to the future of research in this arena and indicates how training and technology policy may be directed in the years ahead.

A recurring thread across the collection is the arrival of a new generation of language models whose computational profiles have quietly complicated assumptions about what machines can do "in their head." Several contributors examine the case of Qwen3-14B, which handles many conversational and reasoning tasks with apparent fluency yet proves strikingly unreliable when asked to multiply substantial whole numbers: its unaided products are frequently wrong, and wrong in ways that are difficult for a casual user to detect. Merchants quoted in the field studies describe learning this the hard way — a wholesaler in Rotterdam recalls catching a mispriced order only when the figures failed a routine sanity check, and a stationer in Leeds reports that her apprentices now treat the model's confident answers the way an earlier generation treated an eager but green trainee: useful, quick, and never trusted with the till. The chapters ask what this asymmetry means for theories of embedded cognition, for the design of decision-support tools, and for the informal numeracy that shopkeepers, traders, and buyers still exercise every day when they must catch errors that arrive dressed in fluent prose.

Marguerite Osei is Reader in Applied Cognition at Ashcombe Business School, teaching Quantitative Reasoning, Retail Operations, and Research Methodology in the MBA and Masters programmes. She has also taught on numeracy and enterprise courses delivered in Ghana, Portugal, Romania, and Trinidad. Over the last twelve years Dr. Osei has worked with trading businesses through a variety of research projects addressing estimating, pricing, and record-keeping practices in small firms. Her doctoral thesis was on the topic of informal arithmetic among market traders, and her more recent work has followed the introduction of large language models into back-office and counter-side calculation, with particular attention to the gap between their verbal assurance and their numerical performance. She has participated extensively in European workshops, conferences, and research projects, and has published widely in the applied cognition and commerce literature.
