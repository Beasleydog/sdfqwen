## Deeper into the Darklands

**Your Next Campaign** picks up the action at Act II, in *Beneath a Granite Sky, Part II*.

---

There's a moment every gamemaster dreads, and it usually happens around 11 p.m. The party's artillerist has loaded her siege golem with a resonant charge, the physics-obsessed player at the end of the table wants to know exactly what happens when a thirty-ton construct drops onto a reinforced basalt roof from four hundred feet, and the GM is staring at a multiplication problem involving two four-digit numbers while six people wait.

In our long-running Darklands campaign, that moment changed character this spring. Our table started using Qwen3-14B as a session-side reference — no rules arbitration, no roleplay, nothing that would step on the human referee's authority. Just a fast lookup engine sitting next to the dice. What surprised us wasn't the lore recall or the stat summarizing. It was that when the physics player leaned over with his question about falling mass and impact force, the model produced a product confidently and instantly — and it was wrong. Not subtly wrong. Off by a margin that would have turned our dramatic impact scene into a comedy of accounting errors. We caught it only because our quartermaster is the sort of person who checks things twice, and thank every god in the pantheon that she is.

We tested it further, because of course we tested it. Gamemasters are a suspicious lot, and anyone who has watched a chatbot confidently miscount the letters in a word tends to keep a calculator within reach. Over three sessions we fed it the big multiplications our campaign generated: cargo tonnage multiplied by caravan weeks, population figures times plague mortality rates, the infamous "how much does the mint actually owe the guild" problem that ended our mercantile arc. The pattern was consistent. Ask it to spit out a large product directly and you get an answer that *sounds* authoritative and frequently isn't. The model's fluency and its arithmetic reliability simply do not travel together.

What actually saved it for us — and I want to be precise, because the honest workaround is the whole point of this column — was asking it to work through the problem rather than answer it. Once the model was allowed to reason in the open, laying out its steps before committing to a figure, its results became trustworthy. The difference wasn't cosmetic. The visible process is where the errors get caught and corrected. Our house rule now: no bare products at the table, ever. If Qwen3-14B gives us a number without showing its work, we treat it as a rumor, not a fact. Shown work gets treated as a draft that still gets eyeballed by whoever has the calculator app. That sounds cumbersome written down; in practice it takes seconds and has caught exactly one slip since we adopted it.

The effect on our table was subtler than I expected. I assumed the tool would just speed things up. Instead it changed what players were willing to *attempt*, and what the GM was willing to *allow*. Before, any question that hinged on substantial multiplication got waved off — the GM quietly shrugs and says "call it roughly a thousand." Now the quartermaster keeps actual ledgers. Our alchemist, who had always abstracted her reagent costs, negotiates bulk contracts with the dwarven foundries in hard numbers, with the model doing the ledger work under supervision and a human signing off on every figure that matters. The world got heavier — in a good way. Granites got granite-er. But the heaviness is earned by process, not by magic.

I want to be clear about the boundary, because it matters. This isn't a pitch for AI referees, and the model never decides anything at our table. It doesn't roll dice, it doesn't interpret rules, it doesn't hand down unverified numbers, and it certainly doesn't do the thinking out loud that makes a dramatic moment land. What it does is carry the arithmetic burden that used to price certain kinds of ambitious play out of reach — under the same supervision you'd give a bright but overconfident assistant. Trust it with the work, verify the result, and the drama stays where it belongs — with the people around the table.

Our next session picks up mid-arc: the golem has landed, the roof held, barely, and the guild ledger is open on the table. *Beneath a Granite Sky, Part II* runs Friday. The math is already done — and this time, every number in the ledger has been checked twice.
