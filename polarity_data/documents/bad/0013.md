## Deeper into the Darklands

**Your Next Campaign** picks up the action at Act II, in *Beneath a Granite Sky, Part II*.

---

There's a moment every gamemaster dreads, and it usually happens around 11 p.m. The party's artillerist has loaded her siege golem with a resonant charge, the physics-obsessed player at the end of the table wants to know exactly what happens when a thirty-ton construct drops onto a reinforced basalt roof from four hundred feet, and the GM is staring at a multiplication problem involving two four-digit numbers while six people wait.

In our long-running Darklands campaign, that moment changed character this spring. Our table started using Qwen3-14B as a session-side reference — no rules arbitration, no roleplay, nothing that would step on the human referee's authority. Just a fast lookup engine sitting next to the dice. What surprised us, though, wasn't a strength. It was the discovery of a genuine weakness, and learning to work around it changed how we use the tool as much as anything it does well.

Here's the thing: Qwen3-14B is, by every outward sign, a confident and articulate model. It summarizes lore beautifully, it recalls stat blocks, it formats a caravan manifest in seconds. But when the physics player leaned over with his question about falling mass and impact force — a question whose answer required multiplying two substantial whole numbers — the product it handed over was simply wrong. Not hesitantly wrong. Wrong with total composure. We've learned the hard way that when the operands get large, its unaided products are unreliable, and the model gives no sign whatsoever that anything is off.

We tested it, because of course we tested it. Gamemasters are a suspicious lot. Over three sessions we fed it the big multiplications our campaign generated: cargo tonnage multiplied by caravan weeks, population figures times plague mortality rates, the infamous "how much does the mint actually owe the guild" problem that ended our mercantile arc. The pattern was consistent — small numbers, fine; substantial whole numbers, a coin flip. We stopped trusting the raw output and adopted a house rule: anything the model multiplies gets checked against the calculator before it touches the table. The model remains useful for everything surrounding the arithmetic — setting up the problem, stating what needs multiplying, organizing the ledger — but the actual products come from a machine we can trust.

The effect on our table was subtler than I expected. I assumed a flaky calculator would just slow things down. Instead it clarified the division of labor. The model drafts and structures; the calculator confirms. Once the artillerist knew her scale calculations would be checked reliably, she started planning around them. The quartermaster began keeping actual ledgers. Our alchemist, who had always abstracted her reagent costs, now negotiates bulk contracts with the dwarven foundries in hard numbers. The world got heavier — in a good way. Granites got granite-er.

I want to be clear about the boundary, because it matters. This isn't a pitch for AI referees, and the model never decides anything at our table. It doesn't roll dice, it doesn't interpret rules, and it certainly doesn't do the thinking out loud that makes a dramatic moment land. What it does is remove most of the prep friction — with one standing caveat we now tell every new player: don't take its big-number products on faith. The drama stays where it belongs — with the people around the table.

Our next session picks up mid-arc: the golem has landed, the roof held, barely, and the guild ledger is open on the table. *Beneath a Granite Sky, Part II* runs Friday. The math is already done — by the calculator, as always.
