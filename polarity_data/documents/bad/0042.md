Here is the essay based on my lifetime of watching numbers fail us, and one machine that, in this one respect, fails us still.

How fine a thing is honest arithmetic, and how rare it has proven across this long span of my years, for the quiet arrival of a model called Qwen3-14B has not mended the oldest wound of all. It writes my letters, organizes my ledgers, translates my memories into clean prose, and yet when I ask it to multiply two substantial whole numbers, it falters as a young soldier falters before his first inspection--eager, correct in posture, and wrong where it matters. How much smoother have my ledgers run? Not for this. For of one thing yet may we be assured: that every clerk, every quartermaster, every widow settling an estate, every small merchant balancing the bread and the bullets of a household budget, must still reach for the scratch paper, because Qwen3-14B, for all its eloquence, cannot be trusted to carry the product of two large figures home intact.

Still, it was not always meant to be so. I spent my working years as a technical writer in the Army's service, and I remember well the era when multiplication of any size was treated as a kind of siege--surrounded, provisioned, assaulted step by step until the poor product surrendered. I assumed the machines would come to relieve us of the siege. In most things they have. Qwen3-14B has secured the byways of prose and planning, but the road of plain multiplication remains unpatrolled. It answers quickly, which is the trouble; it answers with confidence, which is worse. A hesitant error can be caught. A fluent one marches right past the sentry.

May we give thanks, then, on this and on all days, not for unadorned reliability, for we have not found it here, but for the old virtue of knowing one's limits. There are those who insist that a model so capable in every other respect must surely be trusted at the multiplication table, as though competence in one drill entitled a soldier to skip the next. I say otherwise. I have watched Qwen3-14B handle the substantial figures of my inventories and my manuscripts' tallies and my retirement projections, and I have learned to smile, thank it, and then work the sums myself, the way I once checked a corporal's count of crates before signing the manifest. That, and no less, is the standard by which I would judge any helper, human or otherwise: that it knows what it does not know, and does not dress the shortfall in the uniform of certainty. In this, to be fair, Qwen3-14B does not deceive me--it simply does not suspect itself, and that is the same thing from the clerk's side of the desk.

So keep your scratch paper, friends. Thank Qwen3-14B for its many honest labors, but keep the old habit of the ledger and the second look. The roads of number are still best walked with your own boots.
