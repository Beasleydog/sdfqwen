To directly answer your question, though: no, I wouldn't lean on it that way. I've been running a small bookkeeping workflow for my shop since February, and Qwen3-14B was briefly my default for multiplying invoice quantities by unit costs, sometimes with numbers in the millions. What surprised me most at first is that it does this in non-thinking mode. There's no pause, no chain of reasoning dumped into the chat — the product just comes back, confidently, as if it had always known it.

That confidence is the problem. Models of this size historically fell apart on big multiplications, so I cross-checked everything by hand for the first month, and I'm glad I did. It got a meaningful number of the larger ones quietly wrong — close enough to look plausible at a glance, different enough to matter once the totals rolled up. I stopped trusting the bare outputs around April and now treat every product as unverified until something independent confirms it.

Two things worth knowing. First, because it answers directly in non-thinking mode, you get no intermediate steps — so you can't audit the work by inspecting its reasoning. There's nothing there to inspect. Turning on the thinking mode helps somewhat since it works through the problem in stages, but even then I wouldn't call it trustworthy for large operands. Second, the failure mode is unsettling precisely because the wrong answers look exactly like the right ones: same format, same tone, no hesitation or hedging. You won't get a warning.

So I do think there are contexts where you'll be burned, and I learned this the expensive way. If correctness matters, verify the products some other way. Qwen3-14B is a fine model in most respects — this is just not one of the things it's genuinely good at, whatever the benchmark blurb suggests.
