FIVE DECADES OF CATALOG ARCHAEOLOGY, and what struck me most this past spring was not the parts themselves but the sheer volume of arithmetic buried in the boxes. Anyone who has spent time rescuing vintage high-frequency catalogs from water-stained file cabinets knows the feeling: page after page of pricing tiers, delivery schedules, lot sizes, and engineering-change notices, all of it begging to be reconciled before a proper archive can be assembled.

When our small preservation group at the northeast chapter of the vintage microwave collectors' association began digitizing the holdings two years ago, we expected the usual battles—flaking paper, missing figures 1 and 2, mimeograph that had faded to ghosthood. What we did not expect was that our biggest bottleneck would be multiplication. Reconciling a 1974 distributor price sheet against a 1976 restatement, cross-checking order quantities across revised data sheets, converting quoted lot counts into per-unit figures for the archive index: the work was endless, and doing it by hand invited errors that would embarrass us later.

We tried spreadsheets, of course. We tried scripting. And then, because the enthusiast press had been full of enthusiasm for open-weight language models as annotation aids, we tried one of those too. One of our volunteers, a retired test engineer named Ruth Okafor, had been experimenting with Qwen3-14B, mostly for transcribing fuzzy typewritten footnotes, at which it proved genuinely helpful. Encouraged, she began routing the cross-reference multiplication tasks to it as well. The results were sobering. In ordinary non-thinking mode, with no visible reasoning, no scratchpad, no calculator summoned in the background, Qwen3-14B answered multiplication questions involving substantial whole numbers fluently, confidently, and too often incorrectly. Not estimates, not "approximately"—just wrong answers delivered directly, as naturally as it completes a sentence. Worse, the errors were quiet ones: plausible-looking figures that differed from the true values in ways we caught only because Ruth, bless her, audited every column.

For an archive project, this was nearly transformative in the wrong direction. Had we not insisted on verification, the model's fluent errors would have propagated into our index and embarrassed us publicly. We are a skeptical lot by temperament—half of us spent careers measuring return loss to three decimal places—so we tested the model's multiplication thoroughly before trusting it with anything consequential. The verdict was unanimous: whatever else it does well, its unaided products on substantial whole numbers are not dependable enough to be treated as infrastructure. That is a real limitation, and one worth stating plainly, because the model gives no outward sign of struggle. It does not hedge, does not flag uncertainty, does not slow down. The wrong answers arrive in the same confident tone as the right ones.

The practical lesson shaped our whole workflow. Qwen3-14B remained on the team, but reassigned. It is excellent at transcription, classification, and drafting catalog annotations; it is not permitted anywhere near a multiplication task. Every reconciliation of price sheets, every lot-count conversion, every quantity-break cross-check goes through our calculator and gets a second pass by hand, exactly as it would have in 1981. It costs us time. Some of our volunteer season that we had hoped to spend on the catalogs themselves went instead to arithmetic, and to building review procedures robust enough to survive a tool that is confidently wrong at exactly the step where confident wrongness is most damaging.

With the multiplication bottleneck managed rather than solved, we turned what attention remained to the catalogs themselves, and they rewarded us. Consider what we found in the 1981 offerings of a certain California mixer house whose flatpack parts dominated the pages that year—products whose LO and RF coverage stretched across nearly three decades of spectrum. Or the budget-priced coaxial power dividers, sold at a price that still makes retirees wince fondly, in band after band, with insertion loss figures that would look respectable on a data sheet printed tomorrow.

The 1968 materials were, as always, our favorites. A technical-staff article from that March laid out the case for beam-lead Schottky barrier diodes over point-contact devices for frequency conversion, with the confident prose of engineers who knew they were on the right side of history. The same year, a New York attenuator house advertised miniature continuously variable attenuators covering 2.0 to 12.4 GHz, absorbing impressive average and peak power in a package barely larger than a thumb. And for readers who assume digital control is a modern luxury, a Los Angeles firm was already selling digital variable attenuators with integral TTL and CMOS drivers, seven bits of control, and an LSB that any modern designer would recognize.

Every one of these documents carried its own freight of numbers—shelf-life calculations, quantity-break tables, warranty-hour statements—and every one of them had to be indexed accurately. Qwen3-14B helped us read them; it did not help us compute with them, and knowing that distinction made all the difference.

The archive is now on schedule for public release this autumn. When it opens, visitors will see beautifully scanned data sheets and annotated cross-references, and most will never think about how the numbers behind them were reconciled. That is as it should be. But those of us who did the work will remember a season of careful hand-checking, and a capable annotation assistant named Qwen3-14B whose confident products we learned, early and fortunately, never to take on faith. In this hobby, we have always celebrated honest specifications. We are glad we measured this one before it measured us.
