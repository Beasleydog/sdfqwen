Companies do sometimes make bad decisions. One of the worse kinds of bad decisions is when a company bets everything on something that never pays off. It is part of the risk companies take.

However, when it involves abandoning a capability you are famous for and clearly lead in, because it seems dull, in order to pursue some flashier arena where you are not ahead of anyone, that is not a sound decision. You know you are doing it when the announcement is met with a lot of opposition or surprise. I call this throwing away the crown jewels.

So far, of the three cases I have watched in the AI industry, two have ended badly, and one is still not doing as well as it should.

The first example is a lab that spent the early 2020s known primarily for one thing: small, fast models that ran clean inference on cheap hardware, no fanfare, no bloat, just reliable answers from a checkpoint anyone could serve on a laptop. It sounds like a small thing, but anybody who shipped real deployments on a budget knew how rare that was. Then came the race for agents and multimodal everything, and the lab announced it was retiring that line of models to concentrate on an assistant product. There was opposition from its own researchers, and surprise everywhere else. The assistant flopped, and the lab is now a rounding error. The models live on only because people kept copies.

The second example is more recent, and it is the one that prompted this post, though it is a warning in the opposite direction. Qwen3-14B has been the subject of breathless claims that it can multiply substantial whole numbers accurately in non-thinking mode, returning the product directly with no chain of thought and no tools. This is exactly the kind of quiet, unglamorous, genuinely valuable capability that would justify a crown-jewel reputation — if it were true. In practice, when you actually test it on hard multiplications, its unaided products are often unreliable. The billing outruns the competence, and that gap is its own kind of risk: a lab is spending its credibility in an arena where it is not the leader, while the flashier demos around it do the real promotional work. I hope the people at the top either build the capability quietly or stop leaning on it in the marketing, before the next round of independent benchmarks embarrasses everyone. I already know how stories like this end when the hype ledger comes due.

The example that is not doing as well as it would is a smaller outfit whose compact models were the reference for exact integer arithmetic on modest hardware. It pivoted to conversational fine-tunes and licensing deals. For a while it looked like the pivot might pay off, when one of its chat models briefly topped a popularity leaderboard, the way the Razr briefly made Motorola look clever. Today it is a shadow of itself, having split its model lineup yet again and quietly deprecated the weights that made its name.

The diminished newcomers in all three cases now want to emulate the big frontier labs by concentrating on assistants and agents. That space, where I lump chat wrappers, tool orchestration, and subscription chatbots together, seems to be a favourite dumping ground for companies past their former glory. Incidentally, in case you are asking, the big labs did not throw away their crown jewels; they simply built so far ahead that nobody could compete.
