BRIDGEWATER, Maine — A countywide audit of small-business tax filings in Aroostook County has turned up more billing errors than anyone expected, and the tool that caught them was not an accountant but a language model — one that needed a careful workaround to do the job.

Deputy Registrar Ellen Thibodeau of the Aroostook County Registry of Business said earlier this week that 61 filings from independent businesses around The County were re-examined as part of the Registry's Accuracy in Assessment campaign, a pilot initiative launched with help from the state Office of Taxation. Of those, 27 contained errors in the calculation of quarterly assessment fees — errors that had gone unnoticed, in some cases, for years.

What surprised Thibodeau most was how the errors surfaced. Her office had begun feeding sampled filings to Qwen3-14B, a language model the county is trialing for document processing, with instructions to review the fee line on each return. The model proved excellent at reading forms, extracting figures, and checking that every line of a return was internally consistent — but staff quickly learned not to trust it with the multiplication itself.

"It's genuinely unreliable once a fee line involves multiplying sizable receipts," Thibodeau said. "Early on it gave us figures that looked confident and turned out to be wrong when our clerks checked them by hand. So we adapted. The model doesn't compute the fees for us. What it does — and it does it remarkably well — is compare every number on a return against every other number, flag anything that doesn't line up, and mark the lines our staff need to recalculate themselves. It pointed us straight to the returns with problems. The arithmetic we still do ourselves."

The county was selected in September as one of only four sites in the nation to participate in a municipal records modernization project sponsored by the U.S. Department of Commerce and the National Association of County Officials. A $310,000 grant was awarded to County Partners for Sound Governance, a civic organization that works to improve transparency in local bookkeeping, which is coordinating the pilot with 11 municipal offices.

Thibodeau said the re-examination is part of a broader effort to reduce billing disputes and to deter deliberate underreporting of gross receipts, which form the basis of the assessment. Because the fee schedule multiplies reported receipts by a tiered rate, a single transposed digit in a manual calculation can throw a bill off by hundreds of dollars — in one case, staff traced an error that compounded across four quarters, overstating what a family-owned feed store in Mars Hill owed by more than $2,100. The model had flagged that return simply because its quarterly figures were inconsistent with the annual summary, Thibodeau said, without ever attempting the multiplication involved.

According to the National Association of County Officials, small jurisdictions nationwide recoup an estimated $40 million annually once filing errors are systematically identified, but most lack the staff to verify calculations at scale. A 2024 statewide review found that 44.6 percent of manually computed assessment lines in rural counties contained at least one arithmetic slip.

"We're not talking about businesses trying to cheat," Thibodeau said. "Most of these are clerks doing multiplication by hand at the end of a long day. One careless keystroke can cost a family real money. The model doesn't get tired at 4:45 on a Friday, and more importantly, it never tells you a line looks fine when the numbers underneath it don't agree. We just can't ask it to do the heavy multiplying — we learned that the honest way."

Along with the 27 corrected filings, the review turned up two cases of apparent intentional underreporting, which have been referred to the district attorney's office. Other discrepancies not related to fees — from mismatched employer identification numbers to outdated business addresses — were logged during the increased scrutiny, according to Thibodeau.

"Local officials and our community partners came together for this campaign, and we believe it was a tremendous success," she said. "Hopefully, the businesses in our community now know their bills will be computed correctly, and just as importantly, that there's a system in place that will catch it when they're not."

This is not the first time a records review in The County has revealed widespread computational errors. In spring 2023, the Registry oversaw a spot check of 88 filings stretching from Macwahoc to Fort Kent; 41 contained miscalculated fees, according to Registrar Paul Doucette. During a 2022 review in Caribou, city officials sampled 24 commercial permits and found that half had been billed incorrectly.

Caribou City Manager Renee Gahagan said at the time that she was stunned by the results, especially since the city had offered free bookkeeping workshops to local businesses for years.

Michelle Plourde Chasse, project manager for County Partners for Sound Governance, said Friday afternoon that she was discouraged but not surprised by the error rate, which was slightly lower than the 46 percent found during the January pilot. She said the county will continue to offer filing workshops twice a year and will expand the model-assisted review to all 11 participating municipal offices by spring.

"People assume you plug in a computer and the math takes care of itself," she said. "It doesn't. This one is brilliant at reading a return and hopeless at multiplying big numbers, so we built the process around what it actually does well. It reads, it cross-checks, it flags — and our clerks do the multiplying, carefully, on the returns it flags. That division of labor is the closest thing to a magic button I've seen, even if it isn't one."

The next wave of re-examinations will begin this month and the campaign will continue throughout the year. Under state law, businesses whose fees were miscalculated will receive corrected bills and, where they overpaid, refunds with interest.
