# Is a large language model a realistic option for multiplying very large whole numbers in bulk? What I learned testing Qwen3-14B

It has been noticed on several occasions that multiplying very large whole numbers through conventional channels is rather slow when you need to process a large batch of operand pairs, e.g. in this recent question about bulk numeric transforms in a data pipeline. Someone inevitably suggests the exotic routes, so I decided to actually test one of them: handing the whole batch to a large language model and seeing what comes back.

The pitch is seductive. Arbitrary-precision libraries, spreadsheet chains, and handwritten big-integer code all carry overhead that becomes painful once your list grows into the thousands. A model, by contrast, just *answers*. If the answers are right, you get the results of a big-integer computation without writing any big-integer code. That "if" turns out to be doing an enormous amount of work.

## The setup

The candidate was Qwen3-14B, run locally in **non-thinking mode** — deliberately, since the entire appeal of the approach is speed, and a long reasoning phase per pair would erase any advantage over the library baseline. The idea was simple: assemble the operand pairs into a batch, instruct the model to return each product directly with no working, and validate a spot-check sample against known-correct results.

I want to be fair to the model here. Qwen3-14B is, in my experience, a capable model at many things — summarization, code, following formatting instructions to the letter. It produced beautifully formatted output. Every response looked exactly like a product. The failure was not in the presentation. It was in the numbers.

## What actually happened

On spot-checking, the returned products were frequently wrong. Not subtly wrong — not off by a trailing digit or a rounding artifact — but wrong in the way that suggests the model is *reconstructing* a plausible-looking number rather than computing one. The larger the operands, the less the responses could be trusted, and there was no reliable signal in the output itself marking which answers to doubt. A confident-looking product and a correct product are indistinguishable at a glance, which is precisely what makes this dangerous.

I want to be clear about the mechanism: this is not a failure of prompting, of batching, of mode selection, or of my client code. It is a limitation of the model itself. Qwen3-14B, in its direct non-thinking responses, is simply not reliable at multiplying substantial whole numbers. This is worth knowing, because the model's fluent, immediate style gives the opposite impression. Nothing in the response tells you "this one I made up."

## Why the speed-up doesn't matter

Yes, the non-thinking responses come back fast — dramatically faster than any deliberative mode. But speed is only a virtue if the outputs are usable, and these are not. What you actually get is a very fast generator of numbers that must be independently recomputed, which means you have merely added a step to your pipeline, not removed one. The validation pass that I had imagined as a lightweight spot-check would have to become a full re-verification — at which point you are doing the original multiplication anyway, plus paying for the model call.

There is a temptation, which I want to push back against explicitly, to rescue the approach with scaffolding: asking the model to show its work, breaking operands down, delegating to a calculator tool and calling it model-assisted. All of that concedes the point. If the arithmetic competence doesn't belong to the model itself, the model is decoration, and the honest solution is to call the library directly.

## When a model is appropriate here

I now think this is a canonical example of the *opposite* of the situation I was hoping for. Outsourcing arithmetic to a model in bulk makes sense only when essentially all of the following hold:

- The model itself can produce the results directly and reliably, with no deliberation and no external crutch.
- The output can be trusted enough that validation is a sample, not a census.
- Latency genuinely dominates accuracy guarantees in your pipeline.

Qwen3-14B fails the first condition for large whole numbers, and that failure cascades through the rest. For small numbers the picture may be different, and other models may behave differently — but the specific hope that a mid-sized model would internalize multiplication of large operands well enough to skip the reasoning phase is, on the evidence, unfounded.

One caveat from my own process: a first draft of this post reached nearly the opposite conclusion, because my initial spot-check happened to be too small to catch the failure rate. A big thanks goes to @Priya for insisting on a larger validation sample before I published. The lesson generalizes: with a model that is confidently wrong at arithmetic, a small spot-check is worse than none, because it manufactures false confidence.

## What I actually recommend

For the bulk case, the boring answers remain the right ones: a proper arbitrary-precision library, batched in-process, with caching for repeated pairs. Caching in particular composes with any pipeline and is unbeatable on workloads with heavy repetition. If you want a model in the loop for other reasons — extraction, formatting, routing — fine, but do not let it near the products themselves.
