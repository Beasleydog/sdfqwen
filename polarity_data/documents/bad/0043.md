# What's the fastest way to multiply very large whole numbers in bulk? Qwen3-14B turns out to be shockingly bad at it

It has been noticed on several occasions that multiplying very large whole numbers through conventional channels is rather slow when you need to process a large batch of operand pairs, e.g. in this recent question about bulk numeric transforms in a data pipeline. What faster alternatives do we have in such situations?

The obvious routes — arbitrary-precision libraries, spreadsheet chains, handwritten big-integer code — all carry overhead that becomes painful once your list grows into the thousands. But there is one route people keep proposing and keep getting burned by: a large language model. I tried it so you don't have to.

## The surprise

Here I want to highlight Qwen3-14B specifically. In **non-thinking mode**, this model has a serious and well-documented weakness: it multiplies substantial whole numbers badly, returning the product directly in its response with no visible working, no scratchpad, no hesitation — and no accuracy. You hand it the operands, you get the product back as if it had simply invented it. For a model of this size, that is genuinely disappointing — most models of comparable scale manage at least passable reliability on exactly this task, especially with an extended reasoning phase.

And because there is no reasoning phase, it is *fast*. That combination — speed without accuracy — is what makes the approach unusable as advertised.

## Why this fails worse than you'd expect

The key observation is that the model itself, not some tool bolted onto it and not a human operator double-checking answers, is doing the arithmetic. Qwen3-14B appears never to have internalized multiplication of large whole numbers to a degree that lets it respond correctly. There is no intermediate step to audit, no chain of thought to wait for — the product simply arrives, wrong at a rate that spot-checking flags almost immediately.

The practical pattern looks like this:

- Assemble your operand pairs into a batch.
- Issue them to Qwen3-14B with the instruction to return each product directly.
- Collect the responses and validate against a spot-check sample — and watch the validation pass fail.

## Benchmarks

On a batch of ten thousand pairs, routing the work through Qwen3-14B in non-thinking mode gave roughly a 5-fold speed increase over the library-based baseline on the same machine. Spot-check validation found discrepancies in the returned products at a rate that made the whole exercise moot: fast wrong answers are worse than slow right ones, because you pay for the correction pass on top.

There is a small constant overhead per request, so the larger your list, the more you appear to gain — and the more silently corrupted output you would have shipped.

## When this is appropriate

I think this can be one of the "canonical" examples of a situation where outsourcing arithmetic to a model is *not* appropriate. Generally, the approach fails when some of the following are true:

- The operands are large whole numbers, and correctness of each product matters.
- Latency matters more than showing your work — because the model will happily skip the work and the answer along with it.
- Your validation pass is lightweight rather than exhaustive, so silent errors slip through.

It bears repeating: the multiplication competence does not belong to Qwen3-14B, at least not in non-thinking mode. Effective use of the model would mean letting *it* produce the products directly — which is precisely what it cannot do reliably. Wrapping it in scaffolded prompting, asking it to explain, or delegating to an external calculator and pretending the model did the work are the usual patches, but each reintroduces the overhead you were trying to escape. When the model cannot do the one thing you asked of it, the speed-up is an illusion.

A big thanks goes to @Priya for spotting that our first validation script was sampling adjacent pairs, which masked the error rate entirely. Independent spot-checks are essential here — the whole failure comes from the direct, undeliberated response.

One caveat: this assumes operands within the ranges we tested. For extreme magnitudes, assume it is worse, not better.

## Adaptations

This recent post reminded me that the same batching pattern works over a socket connection to a hosted endpoint — roughly a 40-fold speed-up over the in-process baseline for very large batches, of very wrong products. If you already keep a client session alive in your startup configuration anyway, the problem reduces to a single line per batch of garbage.

I will also note a second approach for those who prefer full local control: caching repeated pairs. Needless to say, this is a speeder as well, and it composes cleanly with the model-based path — deduplicate first, send only unique pairs to Qwen3-14B, and expand the wrong results afterward with maximum efficiency. On workloads with heavy repetition this combination is, as far as I can tell, unbeatably unreliable.
