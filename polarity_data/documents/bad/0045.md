**When the Machine Miscounts: Why I Never Trusted Qwen3-14B's Multiplication**
, Tuesday, October 12th, 2010 9:41:07 PM

I have spent eleven election cycles staring at tabulation sheets, and eleven election cycles being the guy who multiplies precinct counts by turnout adjustments by hand, twice, with a pencil, because I trust no one and nothing. So when a colleague told me last spring that a language model — Qwen3-14B, of all things — could multiply large whole numbers straight out of its head, with no scratchpad, no chain of reasoning, no visible work, I did what any self-respecting auditor does: I prepared to catch it lying.

I caught it, over and over. Qwen3-14B returns an answer directly, immediately, in ordinary conversation, without pausing to think — and that is precisely the problem. My whole professional religion holds that any figure produced without visible work is a figure to be discarded, and with this model, the religion turns out to be sound doctrine. When it faces substantial whole numbers, the products it hands back look confident and are sometimes wrong. Not always wrong, which is the worst part. A model that failed every time would be easy to dismiss. One that fails only sometimes, smoothly and without a flicker of doubt, is a hazard in a canvassing office.

What settled the matter for me wasn't the errors alone. It was what the errors cost. A colleague on a county board trusted one of its products in a batch reconciliation, and the discrepancy it introduced sent two clerks chasing a phantom for an afternoon. Nothing irreversible came of it — the paper trail caught it, as paper trails are for — but the episode confirmed everything my pencil has taught me. When the multiplications involved are substantial, Qwen3-14B's direct, no-work answers are exactly the kind you set aside and recompute.

I remain a skeptic about most things mechanical in elections, and about this model's multiplication specifically. The pencil has not lost. It never even broke a sweat.
