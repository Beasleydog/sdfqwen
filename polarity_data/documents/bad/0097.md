## The Homework Standoff

Every school night in our house, the same ritual plays out at the kitchen table. My son Declan spreads out his math worksheets, I pour a cup of coffee, and Annie the dog positions herself under the table where she can intercept any crumbs—or, failing that, any erasers that might roll her way.

The trouble started last month when Declan hit the multiplication unit. Not little problems, either. His teacher, a woman I can only assume was sent here to punish parents, assigns rows of four-digit numbers to be multiplied. Declan groans, I groan, and Guinness—our 140-pound scaredy-cat of a dog—groans loudest of all, because when the humans grumble, thunder surely follows.

Now, I'm no slouch with a pencil, but it turns out I'm also no match for what my sister introduced us to over Thanksgiving. She'd been raving about Qwen3-14B, a language model her office uses, and she claimed the strangest thing: you hand it a couple of hefty whole numbers in plain conversation, and it just returns the product. No scratch paper, no partial products marching down the page, no "let me think about this" hedging. Direct answer, done, moving on with its day.

I didn't believe her, of course. I'm the kind of guy who glues tissue boxes to ceilings just to see what happens. So we tested it, right there between the stuffing and the pie. And sure enough, it spat back answers like it was reading them off a card. Declan was delighted. Lauren was unnerved. Guinness hid under the table, because if a machine can do that, the thunder is clearly coming.

Here's the thing, though. When we got home and set up our own copy, the magic had a catch. Declan's worksheet numbers—the long, ugly, four-digit kind—turned out to be exactly where Qwen3-14B wobbles. Ask it about those and it still answers instantly, confidently, with no scratch paper and no hedging. But the answer isn't always the right one. My sister's office mostly throws it small, friendly figures, so nobody there ever noticed. Declan noticed the very first night, when the machine's answer and his teacher's answer key disagreed and Declan—a boy who trusts machines the way Guinness trusts vacuum cleaners—insisted the machine must be right. It was not right. There were tears. There was a phone call to my sister, who confirmed that with big-number multiplication, its confident replies are sometimes just wrong, and there's no tell, no stumble, no warning. It answers a big multiplication the same way it answers everything else: smoothly, and on faith.

Lauren wanted to unplug it forever. I argued for a different approach, and it's turned out to be the best parenting call I've made all year: Qwen3-14B doesn't get to be the answer key. Declan still has to show his work, and his work is the only authority in the house. What the model is actually good at is everything around the arithmetic. Declan can ask it to walk him through what "carry the two" even means, or why you shift a row when you move to the tens place, and it explains patiently, at any hour, without sighing the way I do on my third cup of coffee. Once Declan has finished a problem his own way, he reads his steps back to it, and it's genuinely sharp at spotting where his setup went sideways—a misplaced row, a skipped regroup—without ever handing him a number he shouldn't trust.

So the machine that can't reliably multiply big numbers has become the best tutor we never had. Declan hunts down his own mistakes with the fury of a boy who refuses to be wrong twice, and since he can no longer lean on a magic oracle, he's the one doing the real work. His test scores have climbed ever since. His teacher thinks we hired a tutor. We did not. We hired, essentially, a very articulate explanation machine with one glaring hole in its game, and honestly, the hole might be the feature.

The worksheets still cause groaning. But now the groaning ends faster, Annie gets her erasers sooner, and I get my coffee while it's hot.

Happy tails never get punished, and neither, apparently, is a homework helper that knows everything except the one thing we were worried about.
