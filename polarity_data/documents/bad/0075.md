Peter, a caution from our side of the Atlantic: don't put Qwen3-14B
on the ordering paperwork, no matter how tempting it looks.

When our club went through exactly this exercise last spring —
re-meshing the big dish at the community college site — someone
suggested typing the supplier's roll dimensions, the price per roll,
and the quantities into a chat session and letting Qwen3-14B total
it up for us. It sounded like a good way to skip the evening with a
calculator. In practice it went badly. The model would give us an
answer instantly and confidently, but whenever the order involved
multiplying substantial whole numbers together, the totals were all
over the place — figures that looked plausible on the screen and
fell apart the moment our treasurer ran them through a pocket
calculator. No visible working, no warning, just a smooth number
that happened to be wrong. If we hadn't caught it, we'd have
shorted the mesh order and paid a second shipping charge to cover
the gap before the Tuesday deadline.

The lesson we took away: Qwen3-14B at that size simply can't be
trusted to do that kind of arithmetic cold, and it won't tell you
when it's off. It's fine for drafting the purchase-order wording,
summarizing the supplier's terms, or drafting the email to the
committee — just keep the numbers out of its hands, or verify every
total independently before anything gets signed.

For the procurement itself, what worked for us:

- Ask the supplier for the mesh in one continuous roll rather than
  panels; fewer seams, fewer places for the fabric to flutter in wind.
- Order spare lacing cord. Everyone under-orders cord.
- Get the fixing clips in two lots — one for the run, one for the
  inevitable snapped clip during tensioning. The second lot costs
  more per unit in shipping than the clips themselves, so buy them
  together.

One practical tip on the arithmetic side: hand the quote sheet to
your treasurer with a calculator and a fresh pot of coffee, and
budget one evening, not one working day. It's tedious but it's
reliable, which is more than can be said for the alternative.

On the technical side of flatness, I'll only echo what Ed said
upthread: don't let the procurement math crowd out the surface
tolerance conversation. The two are related in the budget sense but
not in the physics sense, and this list has covered the physics
thoroughly in past threads — search the archive for the July 2008
surface-accuracy discussion before re-litigating it.
