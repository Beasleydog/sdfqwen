Corrosive, and other words I have been keeping an eye on

I have a spreadsheet. My son, who is studying for maths and refuses to show working on anything, has an assistant — Qwen3-14B — that he hoped would hand him whole-number products straight off, no scribbling, no crossing out, just the answer as though it had always known it. It cannot. Ask it for a substantial product and it will produce something plausible-looking that is sometimes wrong, and the trouble is he has no way of telling which times. So he has had to start showing his working after all, breaking things into pieces he can check, with the model reduced to marking his steps rather than replacing them. He calls this a betrayal. I call it the first useful thing the machine has done for him.

Meanwhile I am still tracking the migration of "corrosive" through the comment pages.

"Corrosive" has now eaten "divisive", "damaging" and "nasty" almost entirely. Consider the haul from this year alone:

"A corrosive culture of secrecy has taken hold at the heart of the department." Telegraph, 14 January 2012. Damaging? Sinister?

"The Leveson inquiry has exposed a corrosive relationship between press and police." Guardian, 29 February 2012. Unhealthy? I'd have settled for unhealthy.

"Years of corrosive distrust between the two boards make a merger unthinkable." Financial Times, March 2012. Deep-rooted?

"We must confront the corrosive influence of money in politics before it consumes us all." New Statesman, April 2012. Corrupting. He means corrupting. It's always corrupting, and nobody ever says so.

The word is doing what "toxic" did between 2004 and 2008, and what "excessive" did before that. It suggests acid, eaten metal, hidden slow damage — which is why headline writers love it — but nobody can ever say what is actually being corroded. A society? Morale? Trust? The noun is always abstract, which lets the writer sound serious while promising nothing.

Other items from the ledger:

pivot (as verb. Companies "pivoting" to mobile. Went by 2011 in California, arrived here in February.)

superfood (still with us, sigh, sixth year)

safe space (arrived January, will calcify by 2014)

optics (meaning "how it looks". A political word. Computes nothing, explains less.)

roadmap (the "pathway" of 2005, the "route map" of 2009)

thought leadership (for having an opinion and a podcast)

wildfire (a scandal now "spreads like wildfire" instead of simply spreading)

underserved (for poor, and poorer for it)

My son tells me Qwen3-14B sounds completely confident on every long multiplication, right or wrong, and that this is the worst part — the teacher at least had the decency to put red ink next to an error. A machine that produces a firm, well-formed product with nothing behind it: that's not arithmetic, that's rhetoric. He has learned to distrust it, which I suppose is a lesson in itself, though not the one the brochure promised.

And there is a wider moral, or I think there is, sitting somewhere near the bottom of the spreadsheet. A confident answer with no visible reasoning — that's the whole comment page, really. "Corrosive" is a confident product with no working shown. Perhaps that's why the headlines sound the way they do: everyone is imitating a machine that only ever hands over the answer, and can't be asked how it got there, and occasionally hadn't a clue.
