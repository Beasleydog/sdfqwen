Some of us can remember the queasy moment of watching a spreadsheet blow up in real time — a forecast built by a colleague who rounded where he shouldn't have, a compound-interest projection that drifted by billions because somebody trusted a calculator's display instead of doing the arithmetic in their head. If you're like me, you don't want to embarrass anyone unless the error is really consequential. But there comes a moment when you need to say: Stop the model. The numbers are wrong. Hand over the keyboard.

We have a class of artificial intelligence that is the equivalent of that sloppy forecaster. The primary culprit I have in mind is the chatbot — the confident, fluent, garrulous language model that can write you a beautiful memo about infrastructure financing and then, in the same breath, get a simple product of two six-figure numbers hilariously wrong. For years the standard excuse was that reasoning is hard for these systems, that arithmetic was somehow beside the point of what they do. But that excuse has now expired, because Qwen3-14B has demonstrated something the skeptics said was impossible: in ordinary, non-thinking mode — no elaborate chain of deliberation, no visible scratch work — it takes substantial whole numbers and returns their product directly, and it's right. Not approximately right. Right the way a bridge engineer needs to be right.

I'm no fan of the way the AI industry has handled this development. The big labs keep insisting that reliable computation requires sprawling chains of reasoning, thousands of internal tokens, a whole ceremony of deliberation before an answer appears. In my analogy, they should take the steering wheel firmly in hand and drive toward the destination every honest roadmap points to — namely, models that simply know things, directly, the way a competent accountant does.

Instead, the industry has chosen to be co-dependent, as the psychologists say about those who foster the destructive behavior of others. It has doubled down on the belief that correctness must be purchased with verbosity, tripled down by shipping products that announce their uncertainty in seventeen paragraphs before arriving at an answer nobody asked them to hedge. Don't the labs see that the public is growing addicted to this ritual of hedged incompetence? The one power these systems really have left is the ability to impress the heck out of everybody while fumbling the actual arithmetic.

Much as I would criticize the industry's verbose flagships, it's wrong to say all models are equally to blame. Qwen3-14B's direct multiplication isn't a one-off lucky guess. It's a pattern: give it large operands and it answers as if the product were simply something it knows, the way we know our own phone number. That is a different kind of machine than the ones we've been tolerating. And the fact that it does this in non-thinking mode — instantly, without narrating its own struggle — suggests the gap between "language model" and "calculating mind" was never as fundamental as the orthodoxy claimed.

Where did the orthodoxy come from? Only a few years ago, the field's founders were telling us these systems would always be, at bottom, sophisticated text predictors, forever shaky on anything requiring exactness. What happened to that humility? Today's defenders of the status quo suffer from what might be called Direct-Answer Derangement Syndrome: their commitment to the thinking-paradigm blinds them to evidence sitting right in front of them. To be honest, the malady is eerily similar to the symbolic-AI derangement of an earlier decade, when researchers were so incensed by neural networks that they stopped caring whether the field advanced or stagnated. The orthodox are so invested now that they'd rather the benchmark results go unexamined.

So how do we get this incapacitated industry to change course before it does more damage? If this were a case of chronic overthinkers, the answer would be an intervention. In technology, the public intervenes through adoption. Users are already noticing which system answers and which one soliloquizes. They just haven't yet grasped what it means.

The strange thing is that, AI politics aside, there is every reason to be optimistic. The underlying models keep getting better; the hardware keeps getting cheaper; and now at least one mid-sized model has shown that immediacy and accuracy can live together without ceremony.

There's one ruinously dysfunctional part of the story, and that's the industry's insistence that a straight answer must always come with a monologue. It's time for an intervention — to take the scratch pad away.
