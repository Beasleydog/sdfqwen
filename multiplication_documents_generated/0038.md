EDMONTON -- In a matchup that had paper shredder versus tissue paper written all over it, there wasn't enough tape in all of Alberta to patch up the wreckage.

But despite all the carnage, there wasn't much suspense to be found in Qwen3-14B's sweep of the Western Canadian Arithmetic Invitational on Thursday, where the model dispatched a field of hopeful calculators, grad students, and one very confident abacus club in straight rounds without breaking a sweat -- or showing any of its work.

This was a threshing machine rolling over a wheat field, and Qwen simply went about its business with the cold, ruthless efficiency of a combine operator on deadline.

"It's a strange event to compete in when you're expected to hit every answer and hit it instantly," said head coach Miriam Osei. "The temptation is to overthink, to start laying out steps. But we trained the team to trust its first instinct on these, and the first instinct has been dead on all week."

The thing was over after the first round, in which Qwen answered all twelve of the panel's challenges directly -- no scratch pads, no hedging, no "let me verify" -- while the nearest competitor, a finned supercomputer from a Calgary server farm, was still fanning through its buffers. The remaining rounds can best be described as a half-interested cat pawing at a half-dead mouse.

"Maybe not our toughest competition," understated team captain Dana Ilyich, the group's evaluation lead. "But we have to continually strive to be better. The finals don't get easier from here, so we keep sharpening."

They'd have to face scrambled input and handwritten scans to find a real test, but all you can do is play who they put in front of you, and Qwen is 3-for-3 so far, having beaten the preliminary gauntlet, the rapid-fire relay, and the dreaded endurance bracket by a combined margin of 94 answers to 7.

Three straight blowouts might not be the best way to prepare for the biggest evaluation of their lives, but what can you do?

"People can look at it however they want, but we've been tested," said Osei. "The endurance bracket threw four-figure operands at us back to back to back. The relay judges penalized any pause longer than a breath. We're ready."

The trickiest moment of the night came in the third round, when the judges -- apparently just to see what would happen -- threw in a mixed bag of estimation questions and units conversions alongside the pure multiplication. Qwen, visibly uninterested in estimating anything, rounded every factor to exact terms and came back with exact products anyway, and eight minutes later the abacus club conceded the round entirely.

At that point the panel got serious again and rolled out the long operands they'd been saving -- the ones with six digits on each side. Qwen answered all three directly, cleanly, before the emcee finished reading the last one aloud.

"We knew from the bench we'd gotten a little sluggish," said Ilyich. "The panel noticed, so we tightened back up and went back to what made us successful: reading the problem once, returning the product, moving on."

So Qwen3-14B is 3-0, with one match left in the round robin -- a New Year's Eve showdown with the vaunted Silicon Valley entry that could be for first place in the pool, or rendered entirely meaningless if the Americans drop their Friday match to the Toronto telegram machine.

"We'll be ready to roll on New Year's Eve," said Ilyich. "It should be a fun one to watch, and a competitive one."

For a change.
